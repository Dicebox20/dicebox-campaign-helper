## Executive findings

The current federal treatment of mass-market broadband internet access service (BIAS) is the FCC’s information-service framework under Title I. The FCC’s 2017 *Restoring Internet Freedom* order expressly included satellite-delivered BIAS in its technology-neutral definition and restored BIAS to information-service treatment. The FCC’s 2024 *Second Title II Order* attempted to reclassify BIAS, including fixed satellite broadband, as a Title II telecommunications service. The Sixth Circuit set that 2024 order aside on January 2, 2025, holding that BIAS providers offer an information service; the FCC reports that rehearing was denied and its mandate issued on March 20, 2025. The FCC’s July 2025 DA 25-613 then restored the relevant rule text to its pre-2024 state. The Sixth Circuit proceeding is therefore not the separate July 2026 Ohio Telecom en banc matter discussed below. [FCC 17-166](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/fcc-17-166a1.pdf); [FCC 24-52](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-24-52A1.pdf); [*In re MCP No. 185*, Nos. 24-7000 et al. (6th Cir. Jan. 2, 2025)](https://www.opn.ca6.uscourts.gov/opinions.pdf/25a0002p-06.pdf); [FCC DA 25-613](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-25-613A1.pdf).

The Communications Act separately says the FCC shall determine whether the provision of fixed and mobile satellite service should be treated as common carriage. That statutory instruction does not itself designate all satellite services as common carriers. The FCC’s 2017 BIAS classification covers satellite-delivered retail BIAS, but it does not answer every distinct space-segment, wholesale, mobile-satellite, or other service an operator might provide. A prior FCC decision about 2 GHz mobile-satellite service treated different system components differently; it is not a Starlink classification. [47 U.S.C. § 153(24), (50)–(53)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title47-section153); [FCC 00-302, paras. 92–98](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-00-302A1.pdf).

FCC records describe SpaceX/Starlink as an NGSO satellite broadband provider, and the FCC’s BIAS definition covers mass-market retail internet access over satellite. **Inference:** ordinary residential Starlink internet access appears to fall within that BIAS category. The reviewed primary records did not contain an express adjudication classifying Starlink’s retail offer under Title I/Title II or a Starlink-specific common-carriage determination under § 153(51). This search limit is not proof that no such record exists. [FCC 24-136, para. 185](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-24-136A1.pdf); [FCC 17-166](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/fcc-17-166a1.pdf).

The draft Act’s public-operator status does not resolve the service classification. Section 201(b) authorizes commercial communications subject to applicable law; § 201(c) gives agency treatment for enumerated statutes, not an express Title 47 classification. Section 107(b) asks the transaction plan to address Communications Act §§ 301, 305 and 310, while § 404(a)(1) already requires comparison with public-service regulation and any proposed public-utility classification. A future drafting pass could make the § 107(b) plan expressly address the classification and common-carrier consequences of each service class under § 153 and, where applicable, §§ 201 and 202. This pass makes no bill redline.

## 1. Statutory categories and consequences

Current 47 U.S.C. § 153 distinguishes an information service, telecommunications, a telecommunications carrier and a telecommunications service. A provider is treated as a common carrier under the chapter only to the extent it provides telecommunications services; the same provision separately reserves to the FCC whether fixed and mobile satellite service should be treated as common carriage. The statute therefore requires service-specific analysis and does not make every satellite broadband, transport, or space-segment activity one legal category. [47 U.S.C. § 153(24), (50)–(53)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title47-section153).

Where a service is subject to Title II common-carrier regulation, §§ 201 and 202 impose duties concerning reasonable service/practices and nondiscrimination. Those consequences depend on a qualifying service and applicable FCC treatment; they do not follow solely from owning a satellite network or from the service’s public benefit. [47 U.S.C. §§ 201](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title47-section201), [202](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A47+section%3A202+edition%3Aprelim%29).

## 2. Broadband classification record

- **2017 general rule.** FCC 17-166 restored BIAS to information-service treatment. Its definition covered mass-market retail access delivered by wire or radio and expressly included satellite technology. Its reasoning treated integrated information-processing and transmission capabilities as one BIAS offering. [FCC 17-166](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/fcc-17-166a1.pdf).
- **2024 attempted change.** FCC 24-52 classified BIAS as a Title II telecommunications service and treated BIAS as a mass-market retail offering to the public for a fee. It expressly included fixed satellite broadband within the definition. [FCC 24-52, paras. 126, 190](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-24-52A1.pdf).
- **Court disposition.** In the BIAS appeal, the Sixth Circuit granted review and set aside FCC 24-52 on January 2, 2025, holding that broadband providers offer an information service. The official FCC record states that rehearing en banc was denied on March 11, 2025, and that the mandate issued March 20, 2025. FCC DA 25-613 states the 2024 rules never took effect because of the 2024 stay and subsequent court action, and restores Parts 8 and 20 to the pre-2024 text. [*In re MCP No. 185*, Nos. 24-7000 et al., pp. 3–4](https://www.opn.ca6.uscourts.gov/opinions.pdf/25a0002p-06.pdf); [FCC DA 25-613, para. 2 & n.6](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-25-613A1.pdf).
- **Docket disambiguation.** The July 31, 2026 Sixth Circuit en banc order is in Nos. 24-3133/3206/3252 and identifies FCC Agency No. 23-111. The BIAS merits decision is *In re MCP No. 185*, Nos. 24-7000 et al., concerning FCC 24-52. The former is not a reopening of the latter. This distinction was made from the face of the official court records. [Sixth Circuit order, Nos. 24-3133/3206/3252 (July 31, 2026)](https://www.opn.ca6.uscourts.gov/opinions.pdf/26a0211p-06.pdf); [BIAS opinion, Nos. 24-7000 et al.](https://www.opn.ca6.uscourts.gov/opinions.pdf/25a0002p-06.pdf).
- **Supreme Court docket check.** The official docket page located for Free Press’s extension application shows an extension through August 8, 2025. No separate certiorari docket was located in the reviewed Supreme Court record; this is recorded as a bounded search result, not as proof no petition was filed. [Supreme Court No. 24A1145](https://www.supremecourt.gov/docket/docketfiles/html/public/24A1145.html).

Accordingly, the source record supports the general post-vacatur Title I treatment of BIAS, including satellite-delivered BIAS, but no express Starlink-specific adjudication. The 2025 court opinion did not separately discuss Starlink or the statutory satellite common-carriage proviso. It should not be extended beyond the BIAS category to all activities of an FSS/NGSO operator.

## 3. Satellite-specific limit and Starlink application

FCC 17-166’s technology-neutral BIAS analysis covers satellite internet access. The FCC’s 2024 order also named fixed satellite broadband as BIAS and included it in its attempted Title II reclassification; that order was set aside. Together, those sources support applying the current general BIAS classification to a retail satellite internet-access offer when it meets the functional definition. They do not establish a blanket classification for separate satellite transport, wholesale capacity, direct-to-cell, mobile satellite, or international service. [FCC 17-166](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/fcc-17-166a1.pdf); [FCC 24-52, paras. 126, 190](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-24-52A1.pdf); [47 U.S.C. § 153](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title47-section153).

FCC 00-302 illustrates why different satellite service components should not be collapsed: for the particular 2 GHz mobile-satellite system before it, the FCC treated the space-segment component as non-common-carrier, mobile earth terminals as common-carrier, and gateway/TT&C stations as not requiring common-carrier treatment. That order concerned a different service, band, and record; it does not decide Starlink fixed satellite broadband. [FCC 00-302, paras. 92–98](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-00-302A1.pdf).

**Starlink application (inference).** FCC 24-136 identifies SpaceX/Starlink as a satellite broadband provider relying on an NGSO constellation. A standardized residential internet-access offer of the kind described by the FCC appears to satisfy the general BIAS definition. No reviewed FCC authorization order or agency record expressly resolves Starlink’s classification or the § 153(51) common-carriage treatment of every service component. Transaction-specific FCC/counsel review should verify the exact service, provider entity, offer terms, and any applicable FCC designation. [FCC 24-136, paras. 77, 185](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-24-136A1.pdf); [FCC 17-166](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/fcc-17-166a1.pdf).

## 4. Current transparency rules do not themselves create Title II status

After the 2024 Title II order was set aside, FCC DA 25-613 restored the Part 8 transparency framework. The current 2026 Federal Register text for 47 C.F.R. § 8.1 requires any person providing BIAS to disclose specified network-management, performance, and commercial information and retains the BIAS functional definition. These transparency requirements are a distinct rule; their application to a BIAS provider is not itself a determination that the provider is a Title II telecommunications carrier. [FCC DA 25-613](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-25-613A1.pdf); [91 Fed. Reg. 52260 (Aug. 13, 2026)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-08-13/pdf/FR-2026-08-13.pdf).

## 5. Bill v1.4 crosswalk and disposition

| Draft provision | Existing treatment | Audit result |
|---|---|---|
| § 107(a)–(b) | No automatic authorization transfer; pre-close domestic plan addresses Communications Act §§ 301, 305 and 310 and the FCC–federal spectrum boundary. | Appropriate for licensing/spectrum transfer; does not expressly direct a service-by-service BIAS/common-carrier analysis under § 153 or §§ 201–202. |
| § 201(b) | Permits the Corporation to operate the acquired commercial business, provide commercial communications, collect charges, and requires compliance with applicable federal law and necessary authorizations. | Does not by itself choose the future operator’s Title 47 status. |
| § 201(c) | Deems the Corporation an agency for listed laws and a wholly owned Government corporation for Chapter 91 of Title 31. | No express Title 47 agency, service-classification, or common-carrier deeming rule. |
| § 404(a)(1) | Requires comparison with public-service regulation, including statutory nondiscrimination, service obligations, and any proposed public-utility classification. | Already captures a regulatory alternative; not a transaction-specific FCC determination for the acquired service. |

**Disposition:** No Bill v1.4 redline in this bounded research pass. The evidence supports a focused follow-up drafting decision: make the § 107(b) plan state the classification of each service class and identify any applicable § 153(51), § 201, and § 202 treatment before closing. Any such instruction should preserve FCC authority and should not predetermine the result.

## 7. Scope boundaries and remaining questions

This pass does not determine international § 214 status, foreign permissions, universal-service contributions, state-law preemption, satellite station licensing or transfer, federal-station/NTIA status under § 305, direct-to-cell classification, actual Starlink customer terms, or operational compliance. It makes no estimate of service costs, spacecraft disposal, or deorbit obligations. It does not assess whether any specific service is being provided in compliance with its authorization.

Remaining source questions are whether an FCC filing or order expressly classifies Starlink’s fixed-satellite retail offer; whether the FCC has made a § 153(51) determination applicable to any Starlink service component; and whether the final public Corporation’s services, provider entities, and control facts change any Title 47 analysis. These require an exact service/entity record and, for the future public operator, transaction-specific counsel/FCC review.
