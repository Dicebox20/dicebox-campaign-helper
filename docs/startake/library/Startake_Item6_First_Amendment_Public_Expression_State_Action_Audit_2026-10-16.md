# First Amendment, Public Expression, and Service Access: Item 6 Audit

**Prepared:** 16 October 2026 (UTC)  
**Research cutoff:** 16 October 2026  
**Project:** The Starlink and Stripes Act, Integrated Working Bill v1.4  
**Status:** Targeted primary-source audit complete; formal project acceptance remains pending. No bill text was changed.

## Executive finding

If Congress creates and controls the proposed operator in the manner described in *Lebron v. National Railroad Passenger Corp.*, its corporate form would not insulate it from First Amendment limits. *Lebron* held that a corporation created by special law to advance governmental objectives, with the Government retaining permanent authority to appoint a board majority, is part of the Government for First Amendment purposes. That is a conditional test; this assignment did not review the final statutory charter or transaction structure. [S1]

The current Bill v1.4 §§203, 205 and 207 already propose substantial protections: viewpoint-neutral service rules, a record of government restrictions and data demands, notice and review, expedited emergency review, and direct injunctive relief. The source record supports those protections as prudent statutory safeguards. It does not establish that every service decision creates a constitutional forum, that every customer has a constitutional property interest in continued access, or that the future operator meets a particular telecommunications classification. [S2, S5, S8, S9, B1]

One narrow drafting issue merits counsel review in a later assignment: §203(b) expressly addresses material government “directions,” while *NRA v. Vullo* recognizes that unconstitutional coercion may be conveyed indirectly and need not take the form of a formal written command. Consider whether the recordkeeping trigger should also capture materially coercive requests or threats. This is a clarity recommendation, not a conclusion that the current text is invalid; no redline is included. [S4, B1]

## Controlling rules and limits

### 1. Government status depends on the charter and control

*Lebron* supplies the closest Supreme Court rule for a government-created operating corporation. The Court treated Amtrak as Government for First Amendment purposes because Congress created it for governmental objectives and retained permanent majority appointment authority. The Court did not decide that Amtrak’s challenged advertising rejection itself violated the First Amendment; it remanded that merits issue. [S1]

For a private contractor or operator, *Manhattan Community Access Corp. v. Halleck* identifies limited paths to state action, including performance of a traditionally exclusive public function, government compulsion, or joint participation. Providing a service that benefits the public, or being regulated by government, is not by itself enough. *Halleck* concerned a private public-access television operator; it does not displace *Lebron* where the operator itself meets *Lebron*’s government-created-and-controlled conditions. [S2]

*Lindke v. Freed* adds a narrow attribution rule for a public official’s social-media activity: the official must have actual authority to speak for the government on the relevant subject and must purport to exercise that authority in the challenged posts. The decision concerned an official’s social-media page, not a government corporation’s service decisions. Its useful design implication here is to identify the responsible official, source of authority, and capacity in which the official acted. [S3]

### 2. Government cannot punish a viewpoint through an intermediary

*NRA v. Vullo* distinguishes persuasion from coercion. Government officials may express their own views and seek to persuade; they may not use official power, directly or indirectly, to coerce a private party into suppressing disfavored speech. At the pleading stage, the NRA plausibly alleged that a regulator used pressure on regulated entities to end their business relationships with the NRA because of its advocacy. The Court vacated and remanded; it did not make final findings that the alleged events occurred. [S4]

The decision is relevant to §203(b): a government communication can create constitutional risk even if it is framed as an informal request or conveyed through another actor. The legal test is contextual and concerns a threat of adverse government action intended to punish or suppress speech; ordinary criticism or voluntary persuasion is not automatically coercion. [S4]

A limited national-security comparison is *TikTok Inc. v. Garland*. The Court assumed without deciding that the challenged federal divest-or-ban provisions were subject to First Amendment scrutiny, then upheld their application to TikTok under intermediate scrutiny based on a content-neutral data-collection interest and the record before Congress. That decision is tied to the specific statute, foreign-control facts, national-security record, and expressive social-media platform. It does not authorize viewpoint-based restrictions or establish a general rule for terminating satellite-broadband access. [S6]

### 3. Editorial discretion is function-specific

*Moody v. NetChoice* recognized First Amendment protection when a service selects and curates third-party expression into an expressive product. It required a function-by-function analysis of the challenged platform laws and vacated and remanded because the lower courts had not conducted the required facial analysis. The Court noted that the Texas law before it excluded Internet service providers. The decision therefore does not decide whether ordinary satellite network management is editorial speech or whether the proposed Act can regulate a communications conduit. [S5]

The Bill’s §203(a) regulates service access and pricing when used to reward or punish political viewpoints, association, protected expression, or electoral activity. That is distinct on its face from ordering an operator to select, edit, or carry a particular expressive compilation. If the future operator also offers curated content features, those functions would need their own First Amendment analysis. [S5, B1]

### 4. Process depends on the interest and the facts

For federal action, the Fifth Amendment applies; *Mathews v. Eldridge* makes the required process context-dependent. Its balancing considers the private interest affected, the risk of error and likely value of additional safeguards, and the Government’s interests and administrative burden. It does not impose one fixed hearing procedure for every service decision. [S9]

In *Memphis Light, Gas & Water Division v. Craft*, Tennessee law limited a municipal utility’s termination of service to cause, giving the customers a protected entitlement in the disputed circumstances. The utility failed to provide notice of the available dispute procedure and a chance to present billing errors to designated personnel able to correct them, violating Fourteenth Amendment due process. The case does not hold that every Internet or satellite customer has a constitutional property interest in continued service. [S8]

The Act may create statutory protections beyond the constitutional minimum. Section 205(b) ordinarily requires notice, reasons, and an opportunity to challenge a suspension or termination; §205(e) requires published notice and review rules, including expedited review after emergency action. Section 207 separately gives an adversely affected person a direct action against the Corporation for declaratory or injunctive relief and does not require an administrative complaint before relief from imminent or ongoing harm. Whether a specific customer also has a constitutional property interest remains a separate, fact- and statute-dependent question. [B1, S8, S9]

The 2026 decision in *First Choice Women’s Resource Centers, Inc. v. Davenport* is relevant to timing. It held that a subpoena demanding donor identities caused a present associational injury sufficient for Article III standing, without waiting for state-court enforcement. It did not decide whether the donor-information demand ultimately violated the First Amendment. This supports preserving an early route to judicial review where a government data demand itself creates a concrete and ongoing speech or association injury; it does not automatically establish standing or liability in a different customer-data case. [S7]

## Conclusion and next work

No statutory defect requiring a bill edit was established in this bounded review. The draft already states viewpoint protections, records and review requirements, emergency safeguards, and a direct judicial remedy. The most useful narrow follow-on is to have counsel review whether §203(b)’s record trigger should expressly cover materially coercive informal or indirect communications, applying *Vullo* without treating ordinary government persuasion as unlawful. A second implementation task is to write the §205(e) rules with prompt, accessible, independent review and restoration after restrictions.

No practical item, named topic, or M07 subcategory is marked formally accepted. No common-carrier classification, merits opinion about actual operator conduct, emergency-use review, statutory redline, or independent counsel opinion is claimed. Bill v1.4 remains unchanged. [B1]
