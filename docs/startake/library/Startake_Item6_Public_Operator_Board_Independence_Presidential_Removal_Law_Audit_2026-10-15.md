**Date:** 15 October 2026

**Scope:** U.S. federal appointment, supervision, and removal rules for the proposed seven-member Board under Integrated Working Bill v1.2 §202, with a limited statutory cross-check concerning federal corporations. This report is not a legal opinion or counsel sign-off.

## Executive findings

1. **Bill v1.2 §202(a) uses the strongest ordinary appointment route for Board members.** It provides for nomination and appointment by the President with Senate advice and consent. The Appointments Clause reserves that route for principal officers; *United States v. Arthrex* describes the limited alternative for inferior officers as an appointment method Congress must expressly vest in the President alone, a court, or a Head of Department. ([*Arthrex*, 594 U.S. 1, 7–8 (2021)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/19-1434_new_j4el.pdf))

2. **The existing at-will removal rule in §202(b) is legally better supported than a for-cause rule.** The Supreme Court held in *Trump v. Slaughter* that the FTC’s protection for commissioners serving staggered terms was unconstitutional and overruled *Humphrey’s Executor* to the extent it supported that result. The Court’s stated rule covers officers under the President’s “general administrative control.” A public operator that runs a communications network and administers statutory service duties would likely exercise executive functions. That application to this proposed corporation is an inference; the Court did not decide this bill, a public telecommunications corporation, or Starlink. ([*Trump v. Slaughter*, No. 25-332, slip op. at 25–29 (June 29, 2026)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-332_qn12.pdf?ltem0rvi1id=))

3. **Staggered six-year terms can serve continuity, but should not be presented as tenure protection.** The *Slaughter* majority explained that a limited term for an executive officer can coexist with Presidential removal in the interim. Section 202(b)’s current express at-will rule already captures that distinction. The written explanation to Congress is compatible with that design if it remains informational and does not delay or condition removal, as §202(b) currently states. ([*Slaughter*, slip op. at 29–30](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-332_qn12.pdf?ltem0rvi1id=))

4. **No change to §202(a)–(b) is recommended in this pass.** The current text deliberately preserves Presidential removal authority while providing staggered service, Senate confirmation, expertise requirements, and congressional reporting. Those features support continuity and public accountability without claiming legal independence from the President. The source review does not establish that any text can guarantee freedom from lawful presidential supervision.

5. **The Board’s appointment of the CEO under §202(c) remains a separate, unresolved Appointments Clause question.** The bill makes the CEO subordinate to Board direction and permits the Board to remove the CEO at any time, which supports—but does not by itself settle—an inferior-officer analysis. The record reviewed here does not establish the CEO’s full statutory duties, whether those duties amount to significant authority, or whether this Board qualifies as a “Head of Department” for appointment purposes. Counsel should resolve those points before the CEO provision is treated as cleared. (*Arthrex*, 594 U.S. at 13–18.)

6. **The corporate form does not itself resolve removal law or establish insulation.** Current 31 U.S.C. §9102 requires specific statutory authorization for an agency to establish or acquire a corporation to act as an agency. Section 9101 defines “Government corporation” through the listed mixed-ownership and wholly owned corporations. This pass does not determine the proposed entity’s final classification under Chapter 91 or which provisions would apply; that remains an Item 6 charter/classification task. ([31 U.S.C. §§9101–9102 (House Office of the Law Revision Counsel, preliminary 2026 Code)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A31+section%3A9102+edition%3Aprelim%29); [§9101](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A31+section%3A9101+edition%3Aprelim%29))

## Question and bill text reviewed

Section 202(a) of Bill v1.2 creates a seven-member Board appointed by the President with Senate advice and consent, makes the President designate a Chair, and requires the Board’s collective expertise to cover engineering, security, finance, communications law, consumer and rural service, emergency operations, and civil liberties. It does not require party or provider representation. Section 202(b) supplies staggered six-year terms but makes each member subject to removal by the President, disclaims holdover and for-cause protection, and requires a written explanation to Congress that does not delay or condition removal. Section 202(c) gives the Board authority to appoint and remove the CEO and makes the CEO subordinate to Board direction. These are descriptions of the reviewed bill text, not conclusions about legal validity.

The question is whether these structures can preserve continuity and trustworthy public service while keeping the operator within constitutional executive control. The governing distinction is between **tenure** (how long a stated term runs) and **removal authority** (whether the President may end that service before the term expires). The bill’s present language makes the second point explicit.

## Governing federal authority

### Presidential control of executive officers

In *Seila Law LLC v. CFPB*, the Court reaffirmed a general Presidential removal rule for executive officers and rejected an attempt to treat *Humphrey’s Executor* and *Morrison v. Olson* as a free-standing license for Congress to impose additional restrictions. The *Seila Law* majority left those precedents formally intact at that time, while describing the historically narrow FTC rationale and the limits on extending it. ([*Seila Law*, 591 U.S. 197, 213–23 (2020)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/preliminaryprint/591US1PP_web.pdf))

The law changed decisively in June 2026. In *Trump v. Slaughter*, the Court held the FTC’s for-cause removal provision unconstitutional. The FTC had a five-member, Senate-confirmed commission, staggered seven-year terms, and a for-cause removal restriction—the structural features most relevant to §202(b). The majority found that the FTC exercised executive power, required Presidential control, and overruled *Humphrey’s Executor* if any broader holding remained. It did not define every boundary of executive power. The majority specifically left unresolved offices that do not exercise executive power, possible historically distinctive entities such as the Federal Reserve, and tenure protections for non-Article III courts. (*Slaughter*, slip op. at 25–29.)

The proposed Board would oversee an operator carrying out a statutory public-service mission, including operational, access, continuity, reporting, and emergency-related duties in the bill. **Inference:** that role is more likely to fall within the executive side of the boundary than the nonexecutive or historically distinctive categories the Court left open. The inference rests on the function assigned to this operator, not merely on its corporate label. Neither *Slaughter* nor another source reviewed decides this proposed entity’s exact constitutional classification.

The *Slaughter* majority also treated limited terms as compatible with removal at will between appointment and term expiration. Six-year staggering can therefore preserve board turnover and management continuity without promising that a member remains in office for six years. A for-cause limit, by contrast, would risk reproducing the central defect the Court found in the FTC statute. (*Slaughter*, slip op. at 29–30.)

### Appointments and supervision

The Appointments Clause establishes Presidential nomination and Senate confirmation as the default appointment route for principal officers. Congress may provide another route only for inferior officers, and then only by vesting appointment in the President alone, a court, or a Head of Department. *Arthrex* emphasizes that supervision and accountability matter to the inferior-officer distinction; a formally subordinate label is not enough if an officer exercises final, unsupervised authority. ([*Arthrex*, 594 U.S. at 7–8, 13–18](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/19-1434_new_j4el.pdf))

The Board members’ President-and-Senate appointment method fits the principal-officer route. That appointment method does not protect Board members from removal; *Slaughter* directly concerned Senate-confirmed commissioners and held their for-cause protection invalid when they performed executive functions. The rule also does not erase Congress’s authority to specify the operator’s statutory mission and duties; it limits Congress’s ability to use tenure restrictions to remove executive officers from Presidential supervision. ([*Arthrex*, 594 U.S. at 7–8; *Slaughter*, slip op. at 25–29](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/19-1434_new_j4el.pdf))

The CEO issue is less settled on the current record. A CEO who carries out substantial federal authority could be an officer; whether that officer is principal or inferior depends on the authority exercised and actual direction and review. If inferior, the appointment clause still requires Congress to vest appointment through one of the constitutionally named routes. The bill expressly says the Board appoints the CEO, but this review does not establish that the Board is a Head of Department, nor does the complete description of CEO powers allow a final classification. The provision is **unresolved, not found unconstitutional**. It should be reviewed against the exact final charter and CEO duties before enactment.

### Government-corporation statute

Section 9102 provides that an agency may establish or acquire a corporation to act as an agency only under a law specifically authorizing that action. Section 9101 defines a “Government corporation” for Chapter 91 by reference to its listed mixed-ownership and wholly owned corporations. These provisions make explicit charter language and Chapter 91 classification important. They do not, on their face, supply a removal-law exception for a corporation that carries out executive functions. Whether the bill’s charter expressly identifies the entity’s form and its Chapter 91 treatment remains open in the prior Item 6 evidence record. ([31 U.S.C. §§9101–9102](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A31+section%3A9102+edition%3Aprelim%29))

## Application to §202

### Board appointments and removal

The President’s nomination and Senate confirmation of every Board member provides the standard constitutional appointment process for principal officers. The President’s selection of the Chair from confirmed members leaves the Senate’s confirmation role intact. The bill’s expertise requirements are legislative qualifications; they do not shift appointment away from the President and Senate. ([*Arthrex*, 594 U.S. at 7–8](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/19-1434_new_j4el.pdf))

The at-will removal sentence is the most defensible available choice under *Slaughter* if the Board executes the bill’s service program. The constitutional exposure of a for-cause alternative would be substantial: it would insulate a multimember governing board with staggered terms, closely resembling the structure the Court rejected in the FTC case. This is a risk assessment by analogy, not a holding that the operator and FTC are identical. (*Slaughter*, slip op. at 25–29.)

The six-year terms should remain described as staggered service periods, not guaranteed tenure. The current text already says the President may remove a member at any time and supplies no cause requirement or holdover protection. The requirement to send Congress a written explanation should remain informational: it should not require prior approval, create a Senate veto, suspend the removal, or make the removal effective only after the explanation. Section 202(b) currently states that the explanation does not delay or condition effectiveness. No separate source reviewed rules on this exact reporting sentence; its compatibility assessment is limited to the fact that the current sentence does not condition removal.

**Recommendation:** Preserve §202(a)–(b) as written in v1.2. The text already distinguishes a term of service from a removal guarantee and meets the strongest appointment and removal design supported by the reviewed authority. No operative amendment is recommended from this pass.

### Independence and continuity without tenure insulation

The Board can retain expertise and operational continuity through staggered vacancies, advance nominations, planned onboarding, documented succession, and tested transition procedures. These are policy and administration choices; they do not limit Presidential authority. The bill also can protect the public mission through clear service duties, nondiscrimination rules, customer notice, published performance measures, audited accounts, conflict disclosures, privacy requirements, complaints, written agency determinations, and judicial remedies directed at the Corporation or responsible officials where Congress provides an available cause of action. Those provisions should regulate how the corporation performs its duties without purporting to bar the President from directing or removing executive officers.

The Supreme Court’s Appointments Clause decisions describe a chain of supervision as part of public accountability, not just formal rank. Bill v1.2’s designation of a Board and subordinate CEO should therefore be read together with the substantive delegation and review provisions. Any officer who can make final decisions with significant federal authority outside actual superior review presents a separate appointment and supervision question. ([*Arthrex*, 594 U.S. at 13–18](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/19-1434_new_j4el.pdf))

## Contrary view and limits

The strongest contrary position is that the Board’s public-service and technical functions need independence from short-term political pressure, and that Congress has historically used multimember bodies, staggered terms, and removal limits to support neutral judgment and continuity. Justice Sotomayor’s *Slaughter* dissent develops that view using *Humphrey’s Executor*, *Wiener*, *Morrison*, historical practice, and reliance interests in independent agencies. It also argues that at-will removal can undermine decisional independence. This is a serious policy argument, but it is a dissent and does not control the result after the Court’s 2026 holding. ([*Slaughter*, Sotomayor, J., dissenting, slip op. at 4–7, 34–36](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-332_qn12.pdf?ltem0rvi1id=))

The majority’s contrary constitutional case is that the President must be accountable for execution of federal law and must retain removal authority over officers within the President’s general administrative control. It rejected the proposition that a multimember expert structure, by itself, justifies for-cause restrictions. It nevertheless reserved a functional boundary for offices not exercising executive power and special historical arrangements it did not decide. This leaves room to argue from the operator’s precise powers and history, but it is not a reliable basis for inserting for-cause protection into §202(b). ([*Slaughter*, slip op. at 18–29](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-332_qn12.pdf?ltem0rvi1id=))

As of the report date, *Gwynne A. Wilcox v. Trump*, No. 26-5, was pending before the Supreme Court and distributed for conference on 16 October 2026. The docket showed no order resolving the petition by 15 October. It concerned removal of a National Labor Relations Board member; its future disposition could affect the litigation landscape, but it supplies no additional holding as of this cutoff. ([Supreme Court docket No. 26-5](https://www.supremecourt.gov/docket/docketfiles/html/public/26-5.html))

## Legislative disposition and remaining work

- **Board appointment:** retain Presidential appointment with Senate confirmation.
- **Board tenure:** retain staggered six-year terms only as continuity terms, subject to the express at-will removal rule.
- **Removal report:** retain written notice to Congress only as an informational duty that neither delays nor conditions removal; no congressional veto or cause requirement should be implied.
- **CEO appointment:** keep open pending a role-specific Appointments Clause review identifying the CEO’s significant-authority functions, level of supervision, and the legally available appointing authority. The present pass does not support either a definitive defect finding or a definitive clearance.
- **Corporation classification:** keep the 31 U.S.C. §§9101–9102 charter and Chapter 91 question open in the broader Item 6 workstream.
- **Bill effect:** no bill edit. This pass does not change the 31 operative sections of v1.2, checklist acceptance, or drafting maturity.
