## Central conclusion

Federal condemnation precedents recognize broad congressional eminent-domain power when properly tied to authorized federal ends and a constitutionally sufficient public use, and public-use case law generally gives legislatures substantial deference. But **a broad public-use conception is not an enacted delegation of acquisition power**, a determination that a specific commercial satellite communications business can be transferred wholesale, a waiver of property-specific just compensation, or proof that an acquisition is necessary. Real-estate and rail cases are analogies, not holdings that current law already authorizes a Starlink seizure. Compare genuine public communications and national-resilience objectives against possible pretext, private favoritism, coercion, operational disruption, and less-intrusive alternatives.

## Competing interpretations and a concrete law-design crosswalk

**Potential public purposes to test (not findings of necessity):** Continuity of emergency satellite communications; nationwide service resilience; civil/military interoperability subject to exclusion of dedicated classified Starshield systems; universal public-service obligations; decreasing single-provider dependence. To support these, obtain independent evidence of actual service failures, measurable coverage gaps, alternative vendor options, resilience benefits, cost, and cybersecurity/privacy implications. These purposes are not interchangeable with a desire to punish a founder or benefit a selected buyer.

**Adverse constitutional and practical arguments:** (1) taking for partisan retaliation or transferring to favored private beneficiaries would raise improper-purpose concerns; (2) negotiated capacity reservations, diversified procurement, targeted regulation and emergency arrangements may achieve objectives with less disruption; (3) multijurisdiction licensing and creditors' rights constrain what Congress can deliver practically; (4) the Fifth Amendment requires identifying property owners and compensable interests; (5) concentrated government control over communications increases speech and surveillance risks, even if public-use threshold otherwise met.
