## Executive finding

No reviewed source establishes that a Starlink public acquisition automatically creates a lawful, secure, neutral, rights-protecting communications operator. Ownership is a *governance choice*, not a cybersecurity accreditation, a privacy law, or a guarantee of uninterrupted service. The governance design must be legislated and independently assessed before any acquisition decision. In particular, federal ownership may create additional accountability mechanisms but also heightened potential for state surveillance, viewpoint interference, political direction, procurement delays, and cross-border friction. Transparent public supervision and effective separation from operational controls are both necessary; neither alone proves adequacy.

## Governance design options and competing risks

**Option A — congressionally chartered government corporation (research hypothesis).** Board with fixed staggered terms, disclosed conflicts, confirmed public-interest directors, statutory operating independence, documented removal grounds, audited financial reporting, inspector-general and congressional oversight, FOIA/records schedules. Benefits: explicit public mandate, transparent audit and formal appeals. Risks: politicized appointment and funding, mission creep, emergency executive influence, commercial sensitivity leaks and procurement constraints.

**Option B — independently regulated private ownership (counterfactual).** Preserve commercial incentives and established operations, add enforceable service continuity, public utility-style duties where legally possible, competition/interconnection and audited resilience requirements. Benefits: avoids extraordinary acquisition/transition costs and state-controlled speech infrastructure. Risks: limited government control over operator failure, ownership concentration and access priorities.

**Option C — public-private trusteeship or targeted service contracting (alternative).** Independent board plus enforceable public-service contracts, escrowed code/interfaces, emergency access and audit rights, without immediate full acquisition. Benefits: potentially reduced transition disruption. Risks: split accountability, vendor lock-in, incomplete access to safety-critical systems. These are alternatives for Item 7; no comparative cost or necessity finding is made here.

## Proposed safeguard design — requirements, not claims of current compliance

1. **Statutory mission/independence.** Require a lawful, narrowly defined communications-service mission; prohibit partisan/geopolitical access manipulation not grounded in law; published nondiscrimination criteria; transparent exception registers with narrowly bounded classified annex. Separate board policy authority from NOC day-to-day incident control. Independent complaint, appeal and judicial-review channels; notice and remedies for wrongful suspension, acknowledging sanctions/export control and host-state legal compliance.
2. **Privacy by design.** Minimize collection/retention of location, traffic, billing and device identifiers; state lawful bases and retention periods; encrypt at rest/in transit with independently audited key management; log privileged access; meaningful account access/correction rights; publish aggregate transparency reports for government requests. Create legal process controls and audit of any national-security or emergency exceptions. Treat foreign data-localization/data-protection duties separately by jurisdiction. The Privacy Act and §222 do **not** cover every category automatically.
3. **Cybersecurity and orbital systems.** Adopt measurable CSF 2.0 functions with verified implementation: asset inventory, segmentation among consumer/commercial/defense networks, spacecraft command-authentication with two-person approval for critical actions, crypto key rotation, terminal firmware signature verification and rollback, supply-chain provenance, independent penetration testing, incident exercises, physical ground-station security, vulnerability disclosure, backup control centers, disaster recovery and tested failover. No assertion is made that any such feature is missing today.
4. **Continuity and critical users.** Map public-safety/disaster/medical/military and ordinary consumer service tiers and contract priorities; publish service-level objectives; preserve launch replenishment, gateways, vendor support and radio rights during transition; pre-fund maintenance and incident response; create migration and redundancy plans with terrestrial/mobile/satellite providers. Test geopolitical and major cyber-disruption scenarios. Rights of foreign customers and host-state licensing must not be assumed transferable by US legislation.
5. **Starshield/classified firebreak.** Perform classified diligence with cleared reviewers and explicit exclusion/transition-service agreements. Do not assume classified payloads, cryptographic keys, mission telemetry, defense contracts or clearance-linked staff transfer. Establish independently auditable logical/physical access boundaries with appropriate classified reporting.
6. **Anti-capture accountability.** Require financial/procurement audits, public pricing/service scorecards, conflict registers, labor safety checks, disclosure of lobbying/contractor relationships, hearings and independent audits. Mandatory redaction rules should protect secrets without preventing oversight. Include whistleblower channels with protection against retaliation.
