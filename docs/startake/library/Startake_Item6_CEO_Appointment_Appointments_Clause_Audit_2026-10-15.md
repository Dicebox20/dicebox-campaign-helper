**Scope:** Article II appointment method and supervision for the chief executive officer (CEO) proposed in The Starlink and Stripes Act v1.2 §202(c). This is not a legal opinion or counsel sign-off.  
**Legal cutoff:** 15 October 2026.

## Executive finding

Section 202(c) is **plausibly constitutional but not cleared**. It directs the seven-member Board to appoint the CEO, allows the Board to remove the CEO at any time, and makes the CEO subordinate to Board direction. Those facts support treating the CEO as an inferior officer and the Board as a permissible appointing authority if the Corporation is a freestanding executive establishment headed collectively by the Board.

The Supreme Court has held that the SEC is a “Department” for Appointments Clause purposes because it is a freestanding component of the Executive Branch, and that a multimember commission may be that department’s head. That holding makes appointment by a statutory board legally plausible here; it does not decide the status of this proposed government corporation. (*Free Enterprise Fund v. PCAOB*, 561 U.S. 477, 511–13 (2010).)

The CEO is likely an “Officer of the United States” if the enacted role carries the ongoing statutory powers described in the bill. The closer question is whether that officer is inferior. A law-created, subordinate role with at-will removal and actual Board direction fits the inferior-officer framework. But this CEO would manage a nationwide communications operator and appoint subordinate personnel. Section 202(c) does not expressly give the Board review or approval authority over material final operating decisions. That breadth and under-specified review leave a meaningful principal-officer risk. This is an application to proposed text, not a holding about Starlink or this Corporation.

**Recommendation:** The bill need not abandon Board appointment on this record. If Congress keeps that route, §202(c) should make the Corporation’s freestanding Executive Branch status and the Board’s collective authority clearer, and describe binding Board supervision over the CEO’s important statutory decisions. Those labels and controls would strengthen the inferior-officer analysis but cannot dictate the constitutional result. If Congress wants the clearest appointment route regardless of whether the CEO is principal or inferior, use Presidential nomination and Senate confirmation; the Board could recommend candidates and supervise operations within the President’s constitutional authority.

## Legal rules and application

### 1. The CEO is likely an officer if the bill’s role is enacted

Article II’s Appointments Clause requires constitutionally prescribed appointment for federal officers. *Buckley v. Valeo* describes officers as appointees exercising significant authority under U.S. law. The Court’s later formulation in *Lucia v. SEC* asks whether a person occupies a continuing position established by law and exercises significant authority under U.S. law. (*Buckley*, 424 U.S. 1, 125–26 (1976) (per curiam); *Lucia*, 585 U.S. 237, 245–46 (2018).)

Section 201 creates a wholly owned Government corporation, treats it as an agency for specified federal statutes, and authorizes it to operate the acquired communications business. Section 202(c) creates the CEO position by law, assigns it continuing management duties, and gives it authority to appoint subordinate personnel. Sections 203–205 assign the Corporation public-service, access, restriction, and customer-process duties. If the CEO executes those powers, this looks more like a continuing statutory office with significant governmental authority than an occasional or ministerial employee role. **Inference:** the CEO should be treated as subject to the Appointments Clause unless the final enacted duties are materially narrower.

The term “CEO” does not itself determine constitutional status. Nor does the Government-corporation label avoid constitutional appointment rules. The conclusion depends on the law-created role and actual powers.

### 2. Inferior-officer status depends on real supervision, not only rank

*Edmond v. United States* explains that inferior officers are generally those whose work is directed and supervised at some level by officers appointed by the President with Senate consent. The Court emphasized meaningful supervisory accountability; it also held that significant responsibilities alone do not make an officer principal. In that case, other executive officers could review the judges’ work, and the judges lacked final authority unless permitted by those officers. (*Edmond*, 520 U.S. 651, 662–66 (1997).)

The factors supporting inferior status here are concrete:

- Every Board member is appointed by the President with Senate advice and consent under §202(a).
- Section 202(c) makes the CEO subordinate to Board direction.
- The Board may remove the CEO at any time.
- The Board acts as a body with a quorum of four serving members; the appointment provision assigns the power to the Board itself.
- The Board’s authority is not merely advisory: it governs the Corporation under §202(a).

These features create a direct, politically accountable superior. Under *Edmond*, the importance of running a large communications operator is not alone decisive.

The weakness is that “subordinate to Board direction” does not say how that direction works. Section 202(c) does not expressly reserve Board approval of the operating plan, budget, material service restrictions, senior appointments, or major contractual commitments, nor does it expressly let the Board review, alter, or rescind material CEO decisions. The CEO’s power to appoint subordinate personnel and broad operating role may leave important authority effectively final. The bill text may imply Board direction, but constitutional classification should not depend on an unenforced label.

*United States v. Arthrex* required correction where executive adjudicators had unreviewable final decision authority inconsistent with their inferior appointments. The Court expressly declined to set an exclusive principal/inferior test and limited its discussion to adjudication; it said it was not addressing supervision outside that context. Therefore, *Arthrex* supports making accountability real, but it does **not** establish a universal rule that a corporate CEO must have each decision reviewed in the same manner as an adjudicator. (*Arthrex*, 594 U.S. 1, 8–19 (2021).)

### 3. A multimember Board may be a Head of Department, if this Corporation is a qualifying department

Section 202(c) says “the Board shall appoint” the CEO. If enacted, that is an express statutory vesting of appointment power. Article II permits Congress to vest appointment of inferior officers in the President alone, courts of law, or Heads of Departments; a statute cannot assign that appointment to an unlisted official merely because that official is subordinate to a department head. (*Edmond*, 520 U.S. at 658; *Arthrex*, 594 U.S. at 7–8.)

In *Freytag v. Commissioner*, the Court rejected the Tax Court as a Department because Congress had made it an Article I court, but expressly reserved whether principal executive agencies such as the SEC are Departments. (*Freytag*, 501 U.S. 868, 885–88 & n.4 (1991).) *Free Enterprise Fund* later resolved the SEC question: a freestanding component of the Executive Branch, not subordinate to or contained within another component, can be a Department for Appointments Clause purposes. The Court also held that the SEC Commission collectively, rather than only its Chair, was the head and that a multimember body may be a Department head. (*Free Enterprise Fund*, 561 U.S. at 511–13.)

The proposed Corporation has features supporting that analogy: it is created by statute, wholly owned by the United States, assigned its own public communications mission, and governed by a Board appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate. Sections 201(c) and 202 make it an agency for specified purposes and give the Board governing authority. **Inference:** the Corporation could qualify as a freestanding executive establishment and the full Board could be its head.

But no cited decision holds that this proposed government corporation is a Department. The statute does not expressly say that the Corporation is an independent establishment in the Executive Branch or that the Board collectively is its head. *Freytag* also cautions that not every part of the Executive Branch is a Department. The corporate form or a statutory declaration alone cannot replace the constitutional, functional analysis.

### 4. Strongest contrary arguments

**Against Board appointment:** The CEO position carries broad, continuing authority over a nationwide federal communications service, including appointment of subordinate personnel. If the Board’s direction is only general policy guidance and it does not actually review or control important CEO decisions, the CEO could be principal rather than inferior. In that case, §202(c)’s Board appointment would use an unauthorized appointing authority. A court might also distinguish the SEC precedent if the new Corporation is not a freestanding Executive Branch component or is legally placed under another agency.

**For Board appointment:** The Constitution does not reserve appointment of every important officer to the President and Senate. *Edmond* says significant responsibility is consistent with inferior status when politically accountable superiors direct and supervise the work. Section 202(c) expressly makes the CEO subordinate to a Board of President-and-Senate appointees and allows at-will removal. *Free Enterprise Fund* is especially relevant: the Court upheld inferior-officer appointments by a multimember commission that it recognized as the head of a freestanding executive department. The seven-member structure itself is not disqualifying.

Neither side has a case directly deciding this combination of a government corporation, a confirmed multimember Board, and a CEO who manages a nationwide communications operator. The right result depends on the final charter and the actual allocation of authority.

## Legislative options

### Option A — Keep Board appointment and define the supervisory structure

If Congress wants the Board to choose and direct management, revise §202(c) to say that:

1. The Corporation is a freestanding establishment in the Executive Branch, not part of or subordinate to another department, subject to the President’s constitutional authority.
2. The Board, acting collectively, is the Corporation’s head for this appointment structure and holds the appointment authority Congress vests in it by law. State this intended structure expressly, while recognizing that statutory wording cannot by itself determine whether the Corporation is constitutionally a Department.
3. The CEO executes Board-adopted policy, operating plans, and budgets; remains subject to binding Board direction; and may be removed by the Board at any time.
4. The Board may review, modify, or rescind material CEO actions and must approve specifically identified matters, such as the annual operating plan, budget, senior executive appointments, and material service-restriction policies.

These provisions would better align the law with *Edmond* and *Free Enterprise Fund*. They would not conclusively make the CEO inferior or the Board a department head; the courts examine the legal structure and actual authority.

### Option B — Use Presidential nomination and Senate confirmation

If Congress prefers to avoid litigating either the CEO’s inferior status or the Board’s Department status, assign the CEO appointment to the President with Senate advice and consent. This is the ordinary route for principal officers and remains available for an inferior officer. The Board may recommend candidates and set policy, but the Act should preserve the President’s constitutional control over execution. This path adds confirmation time and reduces the Board’s control over selecting the CEO.

### Option C — Use the President alone only if Congress deliberately makes the CEO inferior

The Appointments Clause also permits Congress by law to vest appointment of an inferior officer in the President alone. This avoids the Head-of-Department issue but does not solve a principal-officer classification. It is a less conservative fallback than Option B.
