## Executive finding

The public record supports three distinct findings:

1. On 9 October, President Trump publicly said Russia would supply diesel to the U.S. and global market. The Kremlin’s public statement, as quoted by AP, confirmed Russia’s readiness to supply oil and oil products, but did not specify the diesel quantities, buyer, price, payment, or delivery. OFAC separately issued General License 135 on 9 October, authorizing certain Russian-origin diesel transactions under two sanctions regulations, including importation into the United States, through 7 April 2027. Neither statement nor license proves that a U.S. buyer entered a contract, paid for fuel, or received a shipment.

2. Ukrainian officials reported a speed restriction affecting military communications in Ukraine. Reuters published an initial report on 14 October; AP reported on 16 October that a cap had been imposed early on 15 October and cut connectivity above 160 km/h. AP identifies the affected package as Starshield, a U.S.-government-managed military service with separate terms and fees, built on the Starlink service. The public record does not establish that ordinary commercial Starlink customers were subject to the same restriction. Ukrainian officials characterized the decision and operational effects; Reuters separately attributed the order to White House sources. President Trump denied ordering the restriction. No decision order, contract, telemetry, or independent operational-impact record was located.

3. The White House’s Russia-sanctions executive order is dated 16 October, not 17 October. It waives enumerated duties and sanctions/restrictions created by the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026. The order expressly leaves specified pre-existing Russia-related executive-order measures untouched. It is inaccurate to say that the order suspends every Russia sanction. The order says the required certification and report were submitted to Congress; the report itself was not recovered in the public sources reviewed.

The sequence raises a legitimate public-governance and service-continuity question, but timing alone does not establish that the events were coordinated, that a political motive drove the Ukraine restriction, or that any administration or company favored Russia. The current bill already includes evidence-quality, notice, service-review, audit, transition, and redundancy safeguards. This report does not identify a specific bill-text gap; Bill v1.7 and Crosswalk v1.1 remain unchanged.

### Diesel statement and license

The strongest supported description is that Trump publicly announced an arrangement for supply and Treasury issued a general license the same day. AP reports that the White House did not answer who would pay or when fuel would become available. The Kremlin statement, as quoted by AP, confirmed readiness to supply oil and oil products but did not independently confirm Trump’s specific diesel quantities or a completed U.S. purchase. Do not upgrade the reported “tons” to “metric tonnes,” or describe an actual purchase or delivery as verified.

P.L. 117-109, the Ending Importation of Russian Oil Act, separately bans Russian-origin products classified under HTS chapter 27 from U.S. importation “in a manner consistent with” implementation actions under Executive Order 14066. Section 3 provides a statutory termination mechanism requiring consultation, a report and certification to Congress, a 90-day delay, and an opportunity for Congress to disapprove. OFAC GL 135 authorizes specified transactions under parts 587 and 589 and expressly includes U.S. importation. The materials reviewed do not establish that the section 3 certification and effective date occurred, or definitively reconcile the general license with that separate statute. This report does not conclude that the license overrides the statute or that the statute makes the license ineffective; obtain legislative-counsel review before making either claim.

### Ukraine service restriction

The first reports and the described start date differ: Reuters published Ukrainian allegations on 14 October, while AP later reported that the speed cap was imposed early on 15 October. Those dates can describe the first public report and the reported implementation date, rather than a confirmed contradiction about timing.

Product language matters. Some reporting uses “Starlink” as shorthand for a military-grade service. AP describes Starshield as a U.S.-government-managed military package with separate terms, fees, and priority service. Ukrainian officials said the cap affected military terminals and made some drone operations slower or more vulnerable; those are attributed accounts, not independently validated telemetry or mission records. This audit did not find a direct SpaceX technical statement, the relevant contract, government decision documents, or independent logs.

Reuters’ account that the order came from inside the White House rests on unnamed U.S. and Western officials; it is not a source-native order. The Ukrainian adviser’s political explanation is his interpretation. Trump denied ordering the cap. None of these accounts establishes motive or a connection to the diesel announcement or sanctions order.

## Test against Bill v1.7

The current draft already addresses the relevant design risks:

- Section 2 requires a dated acquisition rationale that identifies contrary evidence and uncertainty, distinguishes attributed allegations from observations, and avoids turning disputed incidents into congressional findings.
- Section 3 excludes dedicated Starshield and classified programs from the covered commercial business, while allowing review of necessary interfaces.
- Sections 205–207 require published service terms and capacity rules, ordinarily require notice, reasons, and a challenge opportunity for suspension or termination, require prompt post-action review for emergency restrictions, and provide independent operational/rights audits and judicial remedies.
- Section 401 requires transition-services terms and a critical-service register and excludes Starshield/classified systems from acquisition.
- Section 402 requires witnessed cutover testing and a tested, independently supplied alternative communications path for each critical service, subject to documented feasibility and funded interim safeguards.
- Section 404 requires an independent comparison of service contracts, regulation, emergency authorities, network redundancy, public overlays, negotiated purchase, and compulsory acquisition.

The Starshield incident is not direct evidence that the commercial Starlink assets targeted by the draft were restricted. It is relevant context for the public-interest record and for verifying interfaces, service-control rights, contractual decision authority, and transition dependencies. Existing text covers those concerns. No amendment to Bill v1.7 or Crosswalk v1.1 is recommended by this assignment.
