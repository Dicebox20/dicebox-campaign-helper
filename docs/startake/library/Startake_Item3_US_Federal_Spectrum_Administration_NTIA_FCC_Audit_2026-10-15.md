## Executive finding

Federal spectrum status follows the legal and operational status of each radio station and use. Public ownership of a commercial satellite business does not, by itself, show that a station is “belonging to and operated by the United States,” create a federal frequency assignment, or transfer a commercial FCC authorization. Federal stations use frequencies assigned under the federal assignment framework administered through Commerce/NTIA; commercial/non-Federal operations remain within the FCC licensing framework. Shared-band use requires the coordination and conditions specified by statute, rule, or the applicable assignment. [S1–S5]

Two related statutory tests must remain separate. Communications Act §305 addresses whether a particular station belongs to and is operated by the United States. Section 903(e) instead exempts an “agency or instrumentality” from a narrow requirement to submit proof of an FCC license when using a government-authorized frequency or a U.S.-owned station for a non-government application. That exception is not an affirmative frequency assignment or a general FCC exemption. Bill v1.4 §201 creates a wholly owned Government corporation and gives it agency treatment for listed laws, but does not expressly settle its status under either Title 47 test. The answer therefore remains unresolved for any particular Corporation-operated station until the entity, station, mission, and operating-control facts are established. [S1, S3, S7; Bill v1.4 §§107, 201]

The practical NTIA manual guidance focuses on effective agency control, responsibility for contractor compliance, and supervision sufficient to enforce operating limits. A government agency’s contract with an operator does not automatically defeat federal-station status if the agency retains the required control and responsibility; doubtful cases are to be determined by the agency after consultation with NTIA/FCC as appropriate. This supports a station-by-station determination, not a public-equity shortcut. The manual is agency guidance; it does not conclusively decide whether the proposed Government corporation is an agency or instrumentality for §903(e). [S7–S8]

Public records reviewed show that NTIA led interagency coordination of the FCC’s 2026 SpaceX Gen2 Version 3 proceeding. That is evidence of NTIA participation in an FCC commercial-authorization process; it is not an NTIA assignment to Starlink or proof that Starlink operates a federal station. No public primary source in this bounded review established a Starlink-specific federal frequency assignment or federal-station classification. The absence of such a public record does not establish that no federal assignment or federal-supported operation exists. [S11]

## Question and method

This review tests how federal spectrum assignment and FCC commercial licensing interact with the proposed acquisition and public operation of Starlink. It covers 47 U.S.C. §§305, 902 and 903(e); 47 C.F.R. §§2.102 and 2.103; the NTIA Manual of Regulations and Procedures for Federal Radio Frequency Management (June 2025 edition); the FCC–NTIA Memorandum of Understanding (MOU); and the relevant provisions of the 15 October 2026 working Bill. Sources were checked on 15 October 2026. No Legal Data Hunter, third-party contact, private assignment database, or non-public government record was used.

The official preliminary U.S. Code pages displayed effective-text dates between 23 and 28 September 2026 for the provisions reviewed. The NTIA Redbook is the June 2025 edition. No comprehensive case-law search was undertaken, and this report cites no controlling decision resolving how §903(e) applies to this proposed Corporation.

## Findings

### 1. Federal station assignment and commercial licensing are distinct authorities

Section 305(a) provides a special rule for radio stations belonging to and operated by the United States: those stations are not subject to Communications Act §§301 and 303, and they use frequencies assigned to each station or station class by the President. The statutory language is station-specific. It does not say that a company, system, or asset becomes a federal station solely because the United States owns its shares or acquired its commercial business. Section 305(a) also preserves FCC interference-prevention rules for Government stations when they transmit signals unrelated to Government business, subject to the statute’s vessel exceptions. [S1]

Section 902 assigns NTIA the delegated authority to assign frequencies to radio stations or classes of stations belonging to and operated by the United States, and to amend, modify, or revoke those assignments. It also assigns NTIA policy responsibilities for federal spectrum use. Those provisions identify the federal assignment channel; they do not grant the proposed Corporation a particular frequency or assignment. [S2]

Section 903(e) is a separate proof-of-license rule. A person or entity other than an agency or instrumentality of the United States may not operate a radio station using a frequency authorized for government stations for a non-government application, or use a station belonging to the United States for a non-government application, without submitting NTIA proof that it obtained an FCC license. The provision regulates specified non-government use. It does not itself authorize federal use, assign frequencies, or establish that a government-owned operator qualifies for the exception. [S3]

### 2. The Corporation’s proposed status does not answer the Title 47 questions

Bill §201(a) would create a federally chartered, wholly owned Government corporation, with ownership interests held by the United States. Section 201(c) expressly directs Chapter 91 treatment and deems the Corporation an agency for enumerated laws, including the APA, FOIA, Privacy Act, federal records law, and information-security requirements. It does not expressly deem the Corporation an agency or instrumentality for Title 47, §305, or §903(e). Section 201(d)’s Chapter 91 conforming amendment addresses Title 31 and does not resolve those separate questions. [Bill v1.4 §201]

The adverse reading is that a separately chartered Government corporation is not automatically a department or agency for the station-level §305 test, and that a non-government application could trigger §903(e)’s proof-of-FCC-license rule if the entity is not an agency or instrumentality. The contrary reading is that the United States’ complete ownership and the Corporation’s express Government-corporation charter may support instrumentality status. Neither reading resolves station ownership, operational control, mission, or whether a use is governmental. The available statute and draft do not support a categorical conclusion. [S1, S3, S7–S8; Bill v1.4 §201]

For any Corporation-operated system, keep these determinations distinct:

1. **Entity status:** Is the Corporation an “agency or instrumentality of the United States” for §903(e)?
2. **Station status:** Does the specific radio station belong to and operate under the control and responsibility of the United States for §305?
3. **Application and authority:** Is the use governmental or non-governmental, and is the station operating under a valid NTIA assignment, FCC license, shared-use authorization, or other applicable authority?

A “yes” to one does not establish the other two. Section 903(e)’s exception, for example, would not itself supply a §305 assignment. [S1–S3, S8]

### 3. Shared-band use requires explicit FCC–NTIA coordination

For bands above 25 MHz, §2.102(c) allows a non-Federal station to use Federal frequencies where the FCC finds the use necessary to coordinate Federal and non-Federal activities after consulting the relevant Federal agency. The rule requires FCC–NTIA-agreed conditions, compliance with NTIA service rules, protection against harmful interference, and written Federal-agency certification to the non-Federal licensee; it also provides a separate path under §90.25. [S4]

Conversely, §2.103(a) allows a Federal station to use non-Federal frequencies above 25 MHz, except the listed public-safety bands, only where the FCC finds that use necessary for coordination. The Federal station must follow FCC service rules and FCC–NTIA conditions, protect non-Federal stations from harmful interference, and have written certification from the non-Federal licensee. These are reciprocal coordination mechanisms, not automatic conversion of commercial spectrum rights into federal rights or the reverse. [S5]

### 4. NTIA’s administrative process makes actual control and assignment records material

The NTIA Redbook’s general process has the Federal agency identify a mission need, study and select candidate frequencies, coordinate with affected agencies, and submit an application to NTIA’s Office of Spectrum Management for Frequency Assignment Subcommittee consideration through IRAC. The FCC’s FAS representative submits non-Federal requests in shared bands or where Federal operations may be affected. NTIA identifies IRAC as assisting its assignment of frequencies to U.S. Government radio stations, with the FCC serving as liaison. [S6–S8, S10]

For deciding whether a station belongs to and is operated by the United States, Redbook §8.2.17 asks whether the relevant department or agency can effectively control the equipment and operation; assumes responsibility for contractor compliance with instructions and limits; and provides supervision sufficient to enforce those limits. It recognizes that contracting for operation or maintenance need not diminish federal control, but says doubtful cases should be determined by the agency after consultation with NTIA/FCC as appropriate. Applying that guidance to the proposed Corporation requires facts about the actual station, legal operator, federal agency sponsor, operating agreement, supervision, and the purpose of each use. [S8]

### 5. Current public evidence shows coordination, not a Starlink NTIA assignment

NTIA’s 2026 public statement on the FCC’s approval of SpaceX’s Gen2 Version 3 application says NTIA led interagency coordination with Federal partners. This illustrates the existing FCC–NTIA process around a commercial Starlink FCC authorization. It does not identify a government frequency assignment, establish federal station status, or show that the NTIA coordination transferred to a new owner. [S11]

The reviewed public sources did not establish which, if any, Starlink stations or services currently operate under NTIA assignments, whether particular operations are Federal or non-Federal applications, or whether any federal station is operated under contractor control. NTIA’s federal assignment process and records were not queried or accessed for this assignment. Any closing or operating plan must obtain the underlying assignment, license, agency certification, and contract records from the responsible agencies and license holders; no inference of “none” should be drawn from this report.

### 6. The 2022 FCC–NTIA MOU structures coordination and reached its four-year revisit point

The MOU describes the FCC as the exclusive regulator of non-Federal spectrum use and NTIA as responsible for authorizing Federal spectrum use; the agencies jointly manage spectrum. It establishes planning, staff exchanges, early coordination, data sharing, and dispute-escalation processes. The 2022 NTIA announcement states the agencies would revisit the MOU every four years, placing a revisit around August 2026. An official-source search on 15 October 2026 did not locate a later public successor. That search does not establish whether the review occurred or whether an internal or unpublished successor exists; the current arrangement should be confirmed before the transaction-specific plan is finalized. [S9, S10]

## Application to the Act

Bill §107(a) already states that the Act does not itself transfer a radio authorization, assign frequencies, approve control changes, or establish foreign rights, and requires necessary permissions before affected operations transfer. Section 107(b) requires a pre-closing domestic authorization plan by the Secretary, FCC, and NTIA, including the applicability of Communications Act §§301, 305, and 310; it also expressly rejects an assumption that federal ownership resolves the FCC–Federal spectrum boundary. This is a direct fit for the issue identified here. [Bill v1.4 §107]

No change to §§107 or 403 is supported by this bounded research pass. Section 403 is a foreign-permissions register and is not the operative source for domestic NTIA assignments. The §107(b) plan should include a station-by-station exhibit before closing or any affected transfer, with at least:

- station/system identifier, legal owner, operator, and responsible agency;
- operating purpose and whether each application is governmental or non-governmental;
- each FCC license, assignment, control/transfer filing, or other commercial authority;
- each NTIA frequency assignment or reference, Federal mission sponsor, and any agency certification;
- any shared-band authorization, conditions, interference limits, and written approvals;
- contractor or Corporation operating-control provisions and the official responsible for supervision;
- the reasoned §305 station-status and §903(e) entity/application determinations, with unresolved questions identified;
- the transaction phase or operational change that could trigger a new filing, assignment, or consent; and
- the status of the then-current FCC–NTIA coordination arrangement.

This is an implementation record under existing proposed §107(b), not a new acceptance criterion or a claim that the necessary permissions have been obtained.
