## Executive finding

The authenticated criterion asks about **ethical responsibility when a communications interruption creates identifiable risks**. The reviewed U.S. federal sources do not establish a single, generally applicable legal duty of care that binds every satellite or broadband provider whenever an interruption creates foreseeable risk. The clearest federal service duties examined are conditional: [47 U.S.C. §201(a)–(b)](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-120/t47/s201/) imposes duties on common carriers, while [§153(51)](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-120/t47/s153/) assigns the FCC the determination whether fixed and mobile satellite service is treated as common carriage. These provisions do not, on their face, set a universal outage-free service guarantee or a single restoration deadline. Whether a particular interruption violates an applicable carrier duty depends on service classification, facts, and governing rules; this review makes no such determination.

A distinct ethical conclusion follows from the original scope: where an operator has notice or control over a specific, material risk, reasonable care calls for accurate communication about service limits, proportionate risk reduction where feasible, a documented accountable decision, and no assurance that exceeds the evidence. That is a normative recommendation, not a finding of existing law. The [NSPE Code of Ethics for Engineers](https://www.nspe.org/career-growth/nspe-code-ethics-engineers) provides a relevant professional benchmark for engineers subject to that code; it does not create a company-wide legal duty or bind every communications provider.

Bill v1.14 contains meaningful, risk-based safeguards for the proposed Corporation’s closing and operating-control transition. Sections 104(3), 205, 401(c)–(d), and 402(b), (d)–(e) address executable transition arrangements, service terms and notice procedures, foreseeable-risk registration for contracted critical services, and testing/mitigation at cutover. The critical-service register and cutover tests are phase-specific. The draft does not establish a general post-transition duty of care for every service or a universal continuity guarantee. **No Bill or workbook edit is recommended from this bounded evidence pass.** If Congress intends an enduring post-transition standard, it should set its scope, materiality trigger, duties, exceptions, funding, and oversight in a separate policy decision rather than imply that existing transition provisions already do so.

No actual Starlink interruption, customer, public-service contract, classification, performance, coverage, risk, injury, or provider conduct was established. M04.06, its parent topic, and Practical Item 6 remain formally unchecked. This report is not formal acceptance, legal advice, or professional review.

## Findings

### 1. Federal communications duties depend on common-carrier status and service

Section 201(a) makes furnishing communications service upon reasonable request a duty of a common carrier engaged in interstate or foreign communications by wire or radio; §201(b) requires just and reasonable charges, practices, classifications, and regulations for that covered service. This is meaningful law where its predicates are met. The statutory text does not itself prescribe an across-the-board uptime percentage, an interruption threshold, a notice clock, or a restoration deadline for every communications provider. That observation is a text-based inference; it does not decide whether a particular outage or practice could violate §201 in a covered case.

The satellite qualification matters: §153(51) provides that the Commission determines whether fixed and mobile satellite service is treated as common carriage. The source text therefore does not support assuming that all satellite Internet services are common carriers—or that no satellite service can be one. No FCC service-specific classification or determination concerning the relevant Starlink offering or a future Corporation service was examined. No provider-specific conclusion is made.

**Result:** The federal common-carrier framework is a conditional source of enforceable service duties, not evidence of a universal federal duty of care for every provider or every interruption. Legal application requires the exact entity, service, classification, conduct, and applicable FCC rules.

### 2. Federal tort and constitutional routes do not supply a universal private-provider standard

The Federal Tort Claims Act permits specified negligence claims against the United States where a federal employee, acting within the scope of employment, causes injury in circumstances where a private person would be liable under the law of the place of the act or omission; it is subject to statutory limits and exceptions. [28 U.S.C. §1346(b)(1)](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-118/t28/s1346/). For this project, [§2671](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-118/t28/s2671/) defines covered agencies to include certain corporations primarily acting as U.S. instrumentalities or agencies, while excluding federal contractors; [§2680(a)](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-118/t28/s2680/) includes a discretionary-function exception. Those provisions make entity, employee/contractor, conduct, jurisdiction, and exception analysis necessary. The future Corporation’s treatment and any actual claim are unresolved; the FTCA is not a general duty imposed on every service provider.

In [*DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services*, 489 U.S. 189 (1989)](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/USREPORTS-489/USREPORTS-489-189/context), the Supreme Court held that the Due Process Clause did not require the State to protect a child from private violence in the circumstances before it; the Court distinguished circumstances in which the State has restrained a person’s liberty. This is a limited constitutional boundary, not a ruling about communications providers, common-carrier duties, ordinary tort law, contract obligations, or the proposed Corporation. It does not convert ethical responsibility into a legal cause of action.

No state-law negligence standard was selected or applied. The fact that a risk may be foreseeable is therefore not treated here as sufficient, by itself, to establish a legal duty or breach. A state-specific analysis would require a jurisdiction, relationship, operative facts, service terms, and applicable exceptions. The separate M13.06 audit addresses complaint, appeal, and remedy questions; this report does not repeat its redress analysis.

### 3. Ethical responsibility is broader than the legal triggers identified above

The original criterion is ethical. A useful bounded ethical test is whether the operator had actual or reasonably available knowledge of a **specific and material** risk, whether users or missions reasonably depend on the service for the identified function, what control and mitigation capacity the operator has, whether practical alternatives exist, how severe and time-sensitive the potential harm is, and whether residual risk can be communicated honestly. The strength of the response should be proportionate to those factors. This is an analytical recommendation drawn from the authenticated scope, not a universal legal formula.

The NSPE Code of Ethics for Engineers identifies public health, safety, and welfare as paramount for engineers subject to the code, and calls for notification when an engineer’s judgment is overruled in circumstances endangering life or property. This supports a professional ethics benchmark for covered engineers; it does not establish that all operators, employees, or providers are governed by that code. It also does not make uninterrupted service technically possible or guarantee that any identified alternative will work.

For a public operator, an ethically responsible system should avoid overstating nominal coverage as mission-ready service, communicate material limitations, assess known high-consequence dependencies, consider feasible mitigation, document who accepted residual risk and why, and revisit the decision when the facts change. These are governance recommendations; no evidence here shows that a provider has or has not satisfied them.

### 4. Bill v1.14 addresses transition risks, but does not state a general ongoing care duty

| Bill provision | What the draft does | Scope limit relevant to M04.06 |
|---|---|---|
| §104(3) | Requires funded and executable critical workforce, manufacturing, launch, network-control, software, security, supplier, and customer continuity arrangements before closing. | A closing condition; not a continuing duty for every post-transition service. |
| §205(a), (d)–(e) | Requires accessible published terms, outage notices and complaint procedures; requires a measured access/quality/cost baseline; directs rules on notice, independent review, complaint channels, and restoration procedures. | Transparency and process protections do not themselves set a universal service-safety threshold or guarantee uninterrupted availability. The access baseline measures conditions; it is not a minimum-service entitlement. |
| §401(c) | Requires a transition-services agreement with measurable service levels, incident escalation, response/restoration/cure periods as applicable, remedies, and a funded exit plan. | Applies to the transition-services agreement and covered services; actual terms and mission requirements are unknown. |
| §401(d) | For each proposed phase of operating-control transfer, requires a register of **contracted** services whose interruption would foreseeably threaten life, emergency response, or essential public functions, with mission, dependency, alternative-path, restoration-objective, and accountable-officer information. | This is the draft’s closest express risk-based care mechanism. It is tied to contracted services and proposed transition phases; it is not a universal post-transition rule. |
| §402(b), (d)–(e) | Requires documented tolerances and test criteria before testing; site/service testing; a tested independently supplied alternative path or a documented funded interim arrangement meeting mission tolerances; residual-risk disclosure and accountable remediation. | These controls gate operating-control cutover. They do not establish post-transition availability or a general duty covering all customers. |

**Inference:** The Bill recognizes that identifiable, foreseeable interruption risks require more than a generic promise during transition: it links risk to a register, mission requirements, mitigation, testing, accountability, and funding. At the same time, its detailed care controls are transition-centered and contract-triggered. Sections 205 and 206 provide broader notice and transparency structures, but the cited text does not create a general ongoing duty of care for every service or user. This is a boundary in the draft, not proof of a current-law violation.

## Contrary considerations and limits

- Section 201’s just-and-reasonable standard is broad; this review does not foreclose a regulator or court finding that a covered service practice is unreasonable in a specific case.
- Other federal statutes, state tort law, public-utility duties, emergency-service requirements, valid contracts, and service terms may create additional duties. They were not surveyed comprehensively, and service classification may change the analysis.
- A public operator could have obligations under its enacted charter, regulations, contracts, or employee relationships beyond the sources reviewed. Public ownership alone does not establish the precise legal standard.
- Communications interruptions may arise from hazards outside provider control. An ethical duty to assess and mitigate foreseeable risks does not imply perfect prevention or an absolute restoration guarantee; feasibility, security, lawful restrictions, funding, and safe alternatives matter.
- No incident, customer reliance, actual risk, injury, alternative path, notice practice, provider contract, FCC classification, or technical capability has been established for Starlink or the proposed Corporation.
- The NSPE source is a professional code, not a generally binding statute. No counsel, engineer, provider, utility, school, public-safety dispatch center, customer, or affected community reviewed this report.

## Disposition

**No Bill v1.14 or workbook v0.4 change is recommended from this evidence pass.** The current draft already has bounded transition controls for contracted critical services and separate service-terms/notice procedures. Drafting a universal or enduring “duty of care” without choosing covered services, risk thresholds, feasibility and safety exceptions, funding, post-transition oversight, and enforcement would be speculative and could duplicate or conflict with M04.05 continuity and M04.08 reliability provisions.

If Congress intends an ongoing duty after transition, the remaining policy issue is concrete but unresolved: decide whether it covers all customers or only defined mission-critical services; what level of foreseeability/materiality triggers action; whether the duty is risk assessment, notice, reasonable mitigation, or a combination; how residual risk and exceptions are recorded; who pays; and how compliance is reviewed. Route those choices through a separate drafting decision that reconciles the full-title scopes on continuity, reliability, accessibility, emergency communications, and redress before adding text. This is not a recommendation for an unstated guarantee.
