## Scope and method

This audit addresses the authenticated moral research subcategory M13.06, “Accountability for service harms,” within “Democratic Legitimacy and Public Accountability.” Its exact scope is “Redress, appeal, compensation, judicial processes, and complaint resolution.”

The bounded question is whether the proposed Corporation’s draft Act supplies workable complaint, appeal, and redress routes when ordinary service delivery causes user-facing harm, such as outage/restoration delay, deficient service, or a billing or service-credit dispute. The review compares Bill v1.14 §§205(a), 205(e), 206(a), 206(d), 207, and 501 with current official federal communications law, satellite-outage rules, and government-tort-claim provisions. It distinguishes a complaint channel, an enforceable procedure, judicial review, and monetary relief.

## Executive findings

1. **The draft requires complaint infrastructure but sets few individual service-failure rules.** Bill §205(a) requires accessible, published service terms, outage notices and complaint procedures. Section 205(e) directs the Board to adopt accessible complaint channels and restoration procedures within 180 days. These are meaningful statutory duties, but the text does not set a general service-quality threshold, a deadline to decide ordinary service complaints, a required outage credit/refund, or a measure of monetary compensation. Section 206(d) calls for outage, restoration, complaint and appeal metrics and audits; reporting does not itself provide individual relief.

2. **The Act’s express private action is equitable, not a new damages action.** Under Act §207(a), a person adversely affected by an alleged violation of §§203–205 may sue the Corporation for declaratory or injunctive relief. Section 207(b) makes an administrative complaint optional before seeking judicial relief for imminent or ongoing harm, permits attorney fees, says it creates no damages remedy, and preserves other remedies. Section 501(a) preserves applicable review of final agency action and waives immunity for nonmonetary relief; its savings sentence does not itself specify a damages cause of action. Whether a particular outage violates §205, and whether a plaintiff has standing and can obtain effective relief for a completed service failure, remain fact- and text-dependent questions.

3. **FCC consumer complaints provide a practical channel, but not guaranteed resolution.** The FCC currently offers an Internet complaint category for availability, billing, speed/latency and related issues. For complaints it serves on providers, its guidance describes a written response within 30 days; not all complaints are served or actionable, and the FCC says it cannot resolve every individual complaint. Its formal-complaint process has separate procedural rules and a currently stated filing fee that may change. The FCC process is a useful operational comparator, not a promise that a future Corporation will be within every FCC procedure or that a consumer receives a refund.

4. **Communications Act damages and formal complaints are conditional on common-carrier status and an underlying violation.** Under 47 U.S.C. §206, a common carrier may owe damages and attorney fees for an act or omission violating the Communications Act. Section 207 permits a claimant to choose a Commission complaint or damages suit in district court, but not pursue both. Section 208 permits a complaint to the Commission by a person, public body, municipal organization or State commission, and says the Commission may not dismiss solely for absence of direct damage. These statutes apply to a common carrier subject to the Act and a violation of the chapter; they are not a generic outage compensation rule. The Communications Act expressly leaves the FCC to determine whether fixed and mobile satellite service is treated as common carriage. No provider-specific determination or future Corporation status is established here.

5. **Satellite outage reporting is a public-safety/reporting regime, not a customer remedy.** Current 47 C.F.R. §4.9(c) specifies satellite event thresholds and FCC notices/reports, including certain 30-minute failures, 120-minute notifications, 72-hour initial reports, 30-day final reports, and special 911/988 notifications. The rule is limited by its triggers and service categories; it does not create an individual right to service restoration, a credit, damages, or a consumer appeal. Its existence does not show that a particular broadband outage is reportable.

6. **Tort and contract routes cannot be presumed.** The Federal Tort Claims Act definition includes corporations primarily acting as U.S. instrumentalities or agencies, but applicability to a future operator depends on its legal form, functions, and statutory treatment. An FTCA damages suit against the United States generally requires administrative presentment and final denial or six months without final agency action. Bill §201(c) treats the Corporation as an agency for specified laws, but this review does not decide whether the FTCA covers it or whether an exception applies. Contract credits, refunds or damages also depend on actual service terms; none were supplied. Bill §102(b)’s requirement that certain transition-service agreements specify remedies concerns counterparties, not end-user terms.

**Overall:** M13.06’s core gap is the difference between published complaint procedures and a defined, enforceable remedy for service-delivery harm. The Act creates some procedural and nonmonetary enforcement tools and preserves remedies that may otherwise exist, but it does not define an individual monetary remedy for ordinary outage or service-quality harm. Applicability of existing damages paths turns on provider classification, an underlying legal violation, contracts, and future entity design.

## Findings

### 1. What Bill v1.14 already requires

- **Published service information.** Section 205(a) requires accessible, published service terms, eligibility, prices, capacity-management rules, outage notices and complaint procedures. It also requires service without unlawful discrimination and reasonable disability accessibility. It does not prescribe a minimum uptime, service-level guarantee, outage credit, billing adjustment, or ordinary complaint-decision deadline.
- **Complaint and restoration rules.** Section 205(e) requires Board rules within 180 days after establishment. The rules must specify advance notice for ordinary material adverse changes, expedited notice and independent review after emergency restrictions, accessible complaint channels and restoration procedures. The subsection expressly addresses restrictions and emergency review; it does not state a general time-to-resolution or refund standard for a non-restriction outage complaint.
- **Public performance information.** Sections 206(a) and 206(d) call for quarterly service/outage reports, definitions and limitations, metrics for outage duration/restoration, complaints and appeal dispositions, and independent annual operational and rights audits. These controls support oversight and may help reveal patterns. They do not provide an individual claimant a remedy by themselves.
- **Judicial enforcement.** Act §207(a) authorizes declaratory or injunctive relief for a person adversely affected by a violation of §§203–205. Section 207(b) says an administrative complaint is optional before judicial relief from imminent or ongoing harm, authorizes reasonable fees for a prevailing plaintiff, creates no damages remedy, and preserves remedies otherwise available. The text does not define a customer-only eligibility rule; it uses “person adversely affected.” It also does not resolve how §205(a) applies to a particular service outage, what injury/causation showing applies, or what remedy remains useful after service has been restored.
- **Judicial-review savings.** Section 501(a) leaves final agency action subject to applicable judicial review, waives sovereign immunity for nonmonetary relief for an alleged Act violation, and says it imposes no new restriction on compensation remedies. It does not itself state that every service failure is reviewable agency action or add a new damages remedy.

### 2. FCC consumer complaint channels and limits

The FCC’s Consumer Inquiries and Complaints Center provides an Internet category that includes availability/coverage, billing, speed and latency. The FCC says it may serve a billing or service complaint on the provider; when served, its published process requires a written provider response within 30 days. The process may instead result in FCC educational information or a referral; the FCC warns that not all complaints are actionable or served and that it cannot resolve all individual complaints. A consumer can provide rebuttal information or, subject to the applicable basis and rules, file a formal complaint.

The FCC FAQ states that informal complaints are free, while its current formal-complaint fee is $605 and may change; it also describes a six-month filing window measured from the response to the informal complaint and refers filers to 47 C.F.R. §§1.720–1.740. These details are date-sensitive and are included as process context only, not as a determination that any future Corporation or satellite service would be subject to a particular formal proceeding.

The practical distinction is important: a complaint channel can create a record and prompt a provider response without guaranteeing an FCC order, restored service, a bill credit, or damages. A complaint process alone is not a substitute for a clearly specified statutory entitlement and remedy.

### 3. Conditional Communications Act routes

Sections 206–208 of title 47 describe a stronger statutory route where their predicates are met:

- **Damages:** Section 206 addresses damages and reasonable attorney fees for a common carrier’s act or omission that violates a duty under the Communications Act.
- **Choice of route:** Section 207 allows a claimant alleging damage by a common carrier to seek Commission relief or sue for damages in a competent federal district court, but not pursue both remedies.
- **Commission complaint:** Section 208 allows a person, public body, municipal organization, or State commission to complain to the FCC about a covered common carrier’s contravention of the Act. The FCC may investigate; the statute says lack of direct damage alone is not a basis to dismiss a complaint.
- **Satellite classification:** Section 153(51) provides that the FCC determines whether fixed and mobile satellite service is treated as common carriage. Therefore the statutory remedies cannot be attributed to all satellite internet service or the proposed Corporation without a provider/service-specific classification and a qualifying violation.

The narrow inference from those texts is that an outage alone does not establish a §206–207 damages claim. The claimant would still need an applicable common-carrier duty, a violation, legally cognizable injury and a remedy under the governing process. No such event or status is established for Starlink or the proposed Corporation.

### 4. Satellite outage reporting and user redress

Section 4.9(c) of the FCC’s current outage-reporting rule identifies satellite-specific triggers. It requires rapid FCC notice for specified failures of satellites, transponders, beams or inter-satellite links; specified satellite-access or telephony/paging thresholds; and outages potentially affecting 911 or 988 special facilities. It calls for initial and final reports on stated timelines and for special-facility notifications. It also excludes certain satellite systems used only for private/intra-organizational networks, video/audio distribution, or other non-covered services.

This regime is designed for FCC and emergency-facility reporting. It does not set a general consumer outage-remedy standard, mandate a credit/refund, establish a consumer appeal, or guarantee public access to the underlying report. A reportable-event threshold is not a service-quality standard; the absence of a report does not establish that users suffered no harm.

### 5. Conditional FTCA and contract possibilities

The FTCA defines “Federal agency” to include corporations primarily acting as instrumentalities or agencies of the United States, while excluding contractors. Section 1346(b) provides district-court jurisdiction for qualifying money-damages tort claims against the United States. Section 2675 generally requires the claimant to present the claim to the appropriate agency and receive a final written denial, or wait six months without final action, before suing.

Those provisions make a tort route a question for future entity-specific counsel, not an automatic consequence of public ownership. Bill §201(c) contains specified deeming rules for the proposed Corporation; this review makes no conclusion that it would qualify under the FTCA, that a particular service failure would be a tort, or that an FTCA exception would not apply. The bill itself creates no express end-user tort damages provision in §§207 or 501.

End-user contract remedies are likewise unresolved without actual customer terms. Section 102(b) requires certain transition agreements to specify remedies, but those are negotiated agreements for services needed to operate the acquired business. They do not establish what a retail, public-service, emergency-service or other end user could claim.

## Proposed Act questions for legislative counsel

This review made no bill changes. Counsel should decide, with service users and affected noncustomers identified, whether the Act should:

1. define service-quality/outage and restoration standards or state expressly that Congress is leaving those to later Board rules;
2. set deadlines and notice duties for ordinary service complaints, not only emergency restriction review;
3. state whether outage-related credits/refunds or other monetary remedies are required, and how those interact with the no-damages clause and any remedies otherwise available;
4. identify who may file, what evidence and causation are required, which decision-maker can grant relief, whether an internal appeal is available, and what survives after service resumes; and
5. specify how the proposed Corporation’s FCC status, federal tort status, and customer contracts affect the remedies schedule without overstating any remedy as available.

These are drafting questions, not findings that current text is unlawful or that any particular remedy is constitutionally required.
