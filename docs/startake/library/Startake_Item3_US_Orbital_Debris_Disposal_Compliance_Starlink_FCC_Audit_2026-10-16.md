# Startake Item 3 — U.S. Orbital-Debris Disposal Rules and Starlink FCC Conditions

**Direct primary-source review | 16 October 2026 | Report v0.1**

## Executive finding

The FCC’s current Part 25 framework sets a five-year post-mission disposal deadline for a defined class of LEO spacecraft: stations ending their mission in or passing through the region below 2,000 km and planning uncontrolled atmospheric re-entry must dispose of the spacecraft as soon as practicable, and no later than five years after end of mission. The rule defines end of mission by loss of collision-avoidance maneuver capability, or by completion of the primary mission for a spacecraft without that capability. It is not a universal five-year rule for every orbit, disposal method, or legacy spacecraft. [S2–S4]

FCC applications must also include an orbital-debris mitigation showing. The rules require a disposal-success probability of at least 0.9 for each station; multi-station applications must add information about efforts to achieve more. The 0.99 figure for large systems is a goal, not a categorical FCC threshold. [S2, S4, S5]

For the Gen2 Starlink authorizations addressed in its January 2026 order, the FCC approved modified debris-mitigation plans and imposed specific semiannual reporting duties. If failures result in more than 100 post-failure object-years, SpaceX may not deploy additional satellites until the FCC approves a license modification with an updated plan. These are authorization terms; the order does not prove that any individual satellite is compliant or that the threshold has or has not been reached. [S6]

The bill’s §107 already says the Act does not itself transfer FCC authorizations and requires a pre-close domestic authorization plan. That is a sound framework for preserving regulatory duties, but the plan should expressly inventory each relevant debris-mitigation plan, authorization condition, reporting duty, record custodian, and responsible successor. Section 208 permits qualifying lawful operating and maintenance costs, but it does not name disposal and supplies no cost evidence. The existing Item 5 cost-evidence schedule remains open; this review creates no new schedule or workbook input. [S10, S11]

The FCC adopted a Part 100 replacement for Part 25 in FCC 26-47, but the FCC still described Part 100 as not yet effective on August 7, 2026, and an official September 14 Federal Register rule continued to describe Part 25 as the existing regime until Part 100 becomes effective. I found no later official effective-date notice through this review’s October 16 cutoff. This report therefore applies Part 25 as current law. [S7–S9]

**No fleet or spacecraft compliance conclusion, cost estimate, bill edit, or formal acceptance is made.**

## 1. Scope and method

This is a bounded direct-primary-source review, current through October 16, 2026, of binding FCC orbital-debris and end-of-life disposal rules, publicly available Starlink-specific conditions, and the authorization consequences of a change in operator or control. It distinguishes codified rules, license-specific conditions, adopted rules not yet effective, and prior transition treatment.

The review uses the Communications Act, current FCC rules, FCC orders and the official Federal Register. It does not assess orbital engineering, collision probabilities, individual spacecraft, actual reports filed, constellation-wide compliance, costs, valuation, NEPA, or foreign law. An authorization plan or Commission finding is not treated as proof of operational performance.

Prior Startake reports and ledgers were used only to avoid duplicating adjacent spectrum, NEPA, acquisition-perimeter, and cost work. ## 2. Current binding FCC framework

### 2.1 Disposal-plan and reliability showing

Under 47 C.F.R. §25.114(d)(14), a space-station application must describe the operator’s orbital-debris mitigation strategy and provide required risk and end-of-life information. For the chosen disposal method, the statement must demonstrate a probability of success of at least 0.9 for each individual station. Multi-station systems must provide additional information about efforts to achieve a higher probability; the rule states a goal of 0.99 or better per station for large systems. [S2, S4]

The FCC’s 2024 reconsideration order rejected the argument that the 0.99 goal automatically became a firm requirement for every large deployment. The Commission treated reliability in context with other record facts and retained case-by-case review. The 0.9 showing is a required application demonstration; it is not an observation that a particular spacecraft succeeded. [S5]

### 2.2 Five-year LEO disposal requirement

Under 47 C.F.R. §25.283(e), the five-year deadline applies when all relevant conditions are met:

- The space station ends its mission in, or passes through, the LEO region below 2,000 km.
- The planned disposal method is uncontrolled atmospheric re-entry.
- Disposal is completed as soon as practicable, and no later than five years after end of mission.

For each spacecraft, end of mission occurs when it can no longer conduct collision-avoidance maneuvers. If a spacecraft lacks collision-avoidance capability, end of mission occurs when it completes its primary mission. These definitions make the rule spacecraft-specific even though the authorization and plan are system-level. [S3, S4]

The rule does not prescribe a particular disposal technology. FCC 22-74 declined to create a blanket failure exception. An operator may seek a waiver for good cause after a failure or anomaly, based on the full circumstances; the Commission said waivers would not be liberally granted. [S4]

Section 25.283(c) separately requires a licensee, after the authorized relocation or completion of the mission, to discharge stored energy sources unless prevented by technical failures beyond its control. The specific measures listed include venting excess propellant, discharging batteries, and relieving pressure vessels. This obligation is distinct from the five-year timing rule. [S2]

### 2.3 Effective dates and legacy treatment

The five-year amendment to §25.283(e) became effective September 9, 2024. The Federal Register separately set September 29, 2024, as the compliance date for specified application-information collections, including the updated disposal-success demonstration in §25.114(d)(14)(vii)(D)(1). These dates should not be conflated. [S4]

FCC 22-74 exempted satellites already in orbit from the new five-year requirement. Previously authorized satellites that had not launched received a two-year transition beginning September 29, 2022. New licensees and existing applicants with authorized satellites to be launched after September 29, 2024, must comply; the order also described case-specific waiver consideration and continued processing of pending applications under the then-current rules. [S4]

Accordingly, the five-year deadline cannot be applied to an individual Starlink spacecraft without its authorization, launch date, applicable transition category, approved disposal method, and any later FCC modification or waiver. This assignment did not collect or test those records.

## 3. Starlink-specific authorization conditions

FCC DA 26-36 approved SpaceX’s modified debris-mitigation plan for a first tranche of 7,500 Gen2 satellites and approved a plan for a second tranche of 7,500. The FCC stated that its record resolved concerns it addressed regarding collision risk and failure rates. That is the agency’s licensing determination on the presented record, not an independent audit of actual performance. [S6]

The order’s conditions require SpaceX, for the Gen2 constellation covered by the authorization, to:

1. File semiannual reports by January 1 and July 1, covering the preceding June–November and December–May periods.
2. Report conjunction events and actions; satellites removed from operation or screened from deployment for disposal; atmospheric re-entries; disposal failures, estimated remaining orbital lifetime and cumulative failed-satellite object-years; and collision-avoidance-system outages or unavailability.
3. Stop deploying additional satellites if failures result in more than 100 post-failure object-years, until the FCC approves a license modification with an updated orbital-debris plan addressing failure-rate reduction or risk mitigation. [S6]

The report’s scope is the existence and content of those conditions. It did not inspect all underlying FCC filings, reconcile the semiannual reports, count failures, test the object-years trigger, or certify compliance. The next scheduled reporting date under this order is January 1, 2027, for the June–November 2026 reporting period. [S6]

## 4. Ownership and authorization transfer

Communications Act §310(d) provides that a station license, construction permit, or rights under it may not be transferred, assigned, or disposed of—voluntarily or involuntarily, directly or indirectly, including by transfer of control of a license-holding corporation—except upon application to the FCC and a finding that the public interest, convenience, and necessity will be served. The exact application route depends on the transaction and licensee structure; this review does not identify every Starlink authorization holder or decide how a specific transaction would be classified. [S1, S2]

Bill v1.4 §107(a) states that the Act does not itself transfer a radio authorization, assign frequencies, or approve control changes, and requires necessary permissions before affected operations transfer. Section 107(b) requires a published, transaction-specific domestic authorization plan before closing, prepared by the Secretary, FCC, and NTIA, including the applicability of Communications Act §§301, 305, and 310. [S10]

That plan should name the orbital-debris obligations that the general authorization language does not expressly identify. The recommendation is to include:

- Each FCC station authorization or market-access grant, current holder, docket/file number, and transaction approval required.
- The currently approved orbital-debris mitigation plan, disposal method, modifications, waivers, and applicable legacy transition category.
- Authorization-specific reporting dates, required fields, outstanding filings, and the records needed to support them.
- The responsible post-close operator, reporting owner, records custodian, and FCC filing/modification workflow.
- A continuity control for any deployment pause or other license condition, including the Gen2 100 object-years trigger where applicable.

This is a proposed implementation checklist for §107’s existing plan. It does not presume a transfer, public-interest finding, waiver, or FCC approval, and it does not revise bill text.

## 5. Funding and lifecycle-cost crosswalk

Bill v1.4 §208 limits service receipts credited to the Public Operating Account to lawful operating, maintenance, replenishment, and public-service costs, subject to budget execution, apportionment, audit, and reporting. It does not expressly name orbital-debris mitigation or end-of-life disposal. The present record does not resolve whether a specific disposal expenditure is chargeable under a particular appropriation or how it should be forecast. [S10]

The existing Item 5 comparable/replacement-cost evidence schedule states that current Starlink deorbit, ground-renewal, and other lifecycle unit costs remain unverified. It already provides the cost-evidence request framework. No dollar figure, workbook input, compensation amount, or valuation consequence is supported by this review. The issue remains open for source-backed operational records and separate budget/appropriations analysis. [S11]

## 6. Part 100 transition status

FCC 26-47 adopted a new Part 100 framework to replace Part 25. FCC 26-51, released August 7, 2026, expressly said the newly adopted rules were not yet in effect and continued to use Part 25. The FCC’s September 14, 2026 regulatory-fee rule likewise said it would continue the current approach until Part 100 becomes effective and replaces Part 25. The September 15 erratum confirms that FCC 26-47 included final rules in Appendix A, including Part 100 §§100.260 and 100.261. [S7–S9]

The latest official status located for this review leaves Part 25 as the operative rule set. A future update should verify the effective-date notice and map the final Part 100 text before changing the crosswalk. No draft or circulation text is treated here as binding.

## 7. Findings, open evidence, and status

**Supported findings:** The FCC has a codified, conditional five-year LEO disposal deadline; a per-station disposal-success showing with a large-system goal; legacy transition categories; and additional Gen2-specific reporting and a deployment-pause condition. FCC consent and public-interest review remain a separate statutory step when the transaction transfers or controls station licenses or rights. [S1–S6]

**Open evidence for a transaction-ready plan:** a complete authorization-holder and docket inventory; the latest approved debris plans and modifications for each affected authorization; applicable spacecraft/authorization grandfather dates; actual Gen2 reports and supporting records; any waiver or modification; verified object-years totals and trigger status; and supported lifecycle-cost records. These records were not analyzed at spacecraft or filing level in this assignment.
