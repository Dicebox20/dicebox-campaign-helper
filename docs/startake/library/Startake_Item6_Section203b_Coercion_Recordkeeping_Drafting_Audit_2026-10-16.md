**Project:** The Starlink and Stripes Act, Integrated Working Bill v1.5  
**Claim:** STARTAKE-20261016-WORK-ITEM6-203B-COERCION-LOG-7c4e21

## Executive finding

Bill v1.5 §203(b) requires identification and an auditable record for material government “directions” concerning service restrictions or customer-data disclosure. Section 203(d) logs a material restriction once one occurs. By comparison, the Supreme Court’s coercion cases assess government communications in context—including the official’s authority, what was said or conveyed, and the recipient’s response—and recognize that coercion may be indirect, implicit, or tied to selective enforcement for favorable treatment. *NRA v. Vullo*, 602 U.S. 175 (2024); *Bantam Books, Inc. v. Sullivan*, 372 U.S. 58, 66–68 (1963). [S1–S2]

**Inference from the draft-to-rule comparison:** A material informal or intermediary communication that is not reasonably described as a “direction,” and that produces no completed restriction or disclosure, may not be captured by either existing record. The reviewed cases do not require this Act’s additional reporting system, and they do not make every government request coercive. They support a narrow internal traceability safeguard for communications that, viewed in context, could reasonably be understood to convey a threat of adverse government action—or a promise of selective forbearance—to induce a service restriction or customer-data disclosure in order to punish or suppress protected expression or association.

**Drafting disposition:** Bill v1.6 adds that limited record-preservation and referral trigger in §203(b). The trigger is contextual, material, and tied to a service restriction or customer-data disclosure. It records the communication, apparent authority, requested or threatened action, response, and referral; it does not itself determine coercion or illegality. Ordinary criticism, advocacy, and routine noncoercive coordination are outside the trigger solely because they concern speech. No new government-official cause of action or substantive emergency power is added. The existing §207 remedy applies to violations of §§203–205 against the Corporation; Legislative Counsel should assess how that existing remedy applies to the new recordkeeping duty.

## Primary-source rules and limits

### 1. The relevant test is contextual

In *Vullo*, the Court held at the motion-to-dismiss stage that a First Amendment coercion claim must plausibly allege conduct which, viewed in context, could reasonably be understood to convey a threat of adverse government action to punish or suppress the plaintiff’s speech. The official’s authority, the communication, and the recipient’s reaction are relevant guideposts; no single factor determines the question. The judgment vacated and remanded, so the Court accepted pleaded allegations for that procedural stage rather than finding the events occurred. *Vullo*, 602 U.S. 175 (2024). [S1]

The Court treated a regulator’s alleged conditional leniency and selective enforcement as potentially coercive even though the alleged communication was not a direct command. It also said government officials remain free to criticize views and pursue valid enforcement; the First Amendment bars wielding official power selectively to punish or suppress speech. *Id.*. [S1]

### 2. Informal contact is not automatically coercion

*Bantam Books* involved an informal censorship system in which a state commission sent notices, invoked its referral role, coordinated with police, and the distributor in fact stopped circulation. The Court treated the surrounding authority, communications, follow-up, and recipient response as part of the record. *Bantam Books*, 372 U.S. at 66–71. [S2]

The opinion expressly did not bar all informal government contact: it distinguished consultation genuinely undertaken to help a distributor comply with valid law. *Id.* at 72. [S2] This is an important limit. A statutory log trigger should not treat ordinary criticism, voluntary persuasion, legal advice, or routine coordination as coercion merely because government and speech are both involved.

### 3. The recommended clause is an audit rule, not a constitutional holding

The opinions establish a fact-sensitive First Amendment boundary; they do not prescribe this Act’s internal record fields, Inspector General referral, or review process. Those are proposed safeguards. The standard in §203(b) therefore asks only when a communication should be preserved and referred for review. Preservation does not decide whether a threat was made, whether a constitutional violation occurred, whether any service action was unlawful, or whether a person has standing.

The clause expressly keeps the trigger limited to material communications about service restrictions or customer-data disclosure that could reasonably be understood in context as linking adverse government action or selective forbearance to suppressing protected expression or association. It does not capture a communication solely because it criticizes a viewpoint or asks for lawful cooperation. It does not delay a lawful order or emergency action.

## Existing bill text and proposed addition

### Current Bill v1.5 §203(b)

> (b) Material government directions concerning a service restriction or customer-data disclosure shall identify the directing official, written legal authority, scope, duration and review procedure. The Corporation shall preserve an auditable record, refer apparently unlawful directions to counsel and the Inspector General, and refuse directions prohibited by law. A lawful order may be implemented immediately where delay is unlawful; documentation shall follow promptly where contemporaneous recording is infeasible.

Section 203(d) separately records material restrictions of service, including the decision-maker, affected service and geography, reason, authority, timing, restoration conditions, and review route. Section 207(a) permits a person adversely affected by a violation of §§203–205 to seek declaratory or injunctive relief against the Corporation. Those provisions reduce, but do not eliminate, the identified gap where a coercive communication is not a direction and no restriction follows. [B1]

### Proposed Bill v1.6 §203(b) addition

Retain the existing paragraph and append:

> The Corporation shall also preserve an auditable record of any other material government communication, including one transmitted through an intermediary, whether oral or written, concerning a service restriction or customer-data disclosure that, viewed in context, could reasonably be understood to convey a threat of adverse government action or a promise of selective forbearance from such action to induce the Corporation or another person to restrict service or disclose customer data to punish or suppress protected expression or association. The record shall identify, to the extent known, the official and apparent authority, date and medium, action requested or threatened, authority invoked or the fact that none was identified, the Corporation’s response and disposition, and any referral for review. The Corporation shall refer the communication promptly to counsel and the Inspector General. This paragraph creates a recordkeeping and review duty only; it neither determines that a communication is coercive or unlawful nor enlarges any legal authority. It does not require logging ordinary criticism, public advocacy, or routine noncoercive coordination solely because it concerns speech, and does not delay compliance with a lawful order or emergency action. Disclosure remains subject to applicable law.

**Change control:** Only §203(b) changes from Bill v1.5; the other 30 of 31 numbered operative sections remain byte-identical. Section 203(d), §207, the emergency framework, statutory remedies, the commercial-only acquisition perimeter, compensation protections, and formal acceptance status are unchanged.

**Open drafting question:** Because §207 already covers violations of §203, Congress’s Legislative Counsel should determine whether a person adversely affected by failure to keep or refer a record may obtain the existing injunctive remedy against the Corporation. The text creates no cause of action against an official and authorizes no damages. It does not resolve whether that remedy should apply to recordkeeping failures.

## Contrary considerations and remaining review

- The existing term “government directions” might be read broadly enough to cover some oral or indirect commands, and §203(d) records a material restriction once implemented. The draft-to-rule gap is therefore an inference, not a judicial finding.
- A record mandate that sweeps in ordinary contacts could chill lawful government speech, consultation, and enforcement. The Supreme Court permits persuasion and valid law enforcement; *Bantam Books* specifically preserves genuine compliance consultation. The proposed threshold and carveout address this risk but need Legislative Counsel review.
- The Court’s coercion inquiry is fact-specific. The proposed record is a lead for review, not a presumption that a communication was coercive.
- The proposal concerns the Corporation’s recordkeeping and referral. It does not determine a government official’s liability or create a suit against the official.
- Section 207’s interaction with the new §203(b) record duty is unresolved and should be decided by Legislative Counsel.
- No actual communication, censorship, restriction, data demand, operator practice, regulatory classification, customer entitlement, implementation cost, or independent legal opinion is established.
