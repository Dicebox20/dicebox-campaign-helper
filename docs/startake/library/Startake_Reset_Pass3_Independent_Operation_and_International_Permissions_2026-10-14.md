## Core conclusion and scope

A hypothetical compensated, congressionally authorized purchase of **Starlink communications infrastructure and operations only** could not be treated as day-one autonomous merely by transferring satellites and customer accounts. SpaceX's separately reported launch operations, satellite replenishment, manufacturing/engineering, internal cost allocations, control software and network staff must be converted into **transferred capabilities, enforceable long-term supply/launch arrangements, IP licences, and transition services**, with fallback options and termination protections. The publicly available evidence does **not** demonstrate that a stand-alone Starlink entity could today manufacture, launch and operate the network without continuing support from retained SpaceX. Nor would a U.S. public owner automatically inherit valid market-access or spectrum permissions abroad. Starshield/classified assets remain presumptively **excluded**, with shared interfaces documented instead of presumed transferable.

1. Enumerate **each satellite object, licence, local operator, gateway and relevant spectrum filing** with exact license/registry numbers and current status.
2. Create one legal entity/control chain for each authorization; separately classify asset transfer, corporate share acquisition or bespoke public vesting structure, then check regulator-specific consent and notification requirements.

6. Map customer/enterprise/government service agreements, lawful data transfers, sanctions/export controls and local telecom security requirements; isolate Starshield dependencies.

## Competing interpretations, alternatives and risks

**Case for technical feasibility (conditional):** The functions are identifiable; an acquirer could in principle keep uninterrupted service via staffed operational transfer plus supplier, launch and IP contracts rather than immediately replicating SpaceX's rocket or satellite factories. US government ownership need not automatically require owning all production assets.

**Strongest contrary case:** There is no evidenced arm's-length contract for SpaceX's internally launched satellites; internal transfer-price absence understates potential standalone fleet maintenance cost. Manufacturer dependence, proprietary IP and key personnel, international control/ownership restrictions, and classified Starshield interfaces could make a 'Starlink-only' ownership model fragile or prohibitively expensive. Some host states might not authorize a change in operator or ownership. Other SpaceX customers and governments could contest access to shared supply and infrastructure. **Neither ownership acquisition nor statutory legislation guarantees operational autonomy.**

**Less intrusive alternatives:** Long-term government service agreements with outage provisions, diversified satellite-provider procurement, redundancy/multi-orbit interoperability, regulatory/public-utility conditions where lawful, and continuity/funding covenants. Compare against acquisition once source-backed cost, service resilience and international risks are available.
