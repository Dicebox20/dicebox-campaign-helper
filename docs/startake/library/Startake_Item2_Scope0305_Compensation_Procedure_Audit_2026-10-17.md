## Question and method

This audit examines whether Bill v1.10 §§103, 105 and 106 give a clear process for appraisal dates, the legal date of a taking, court deposits, payment, and any constitutionally required interest for property acquired through the proposed condemnation procedure. It distinguishes the land procedure incorporated by §105(f) from the proposed no-quick-take procedure for tangible personal property. It does not decide whether any Starlink asset is owned by a particular entity, whether any particular interest can constitutionally be taken, or what any asset is worth.

The primary-source review covers the official text of [40 U.S.C. §3114](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title40-section3114) (House Code text reports laws effective September 28, 2026), [40 U.S.C. §3116](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A40+section%3A3116+edition%3Aprelim) (House Code text reports laws effective August 30, 2026), the [Federal Rules of Civil Procedure as amended December 1, 2025](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-civil-procedure.pdf), and the official U.S. Reports decisions [United States v. ## Findings

### 1. Taking date and valuation date are related, but not interchangeable

In most cases, constitutional just compensation is measured by the property’s fair market value at the time it is taken, while other measures may apply where market value is too difficult to determine or would produce manifest injustice. [Miller](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-317/pdf/USREPORTS-317-369.pdf) states the valuation-date rule and recognizes the project-influence limit; [Kirby Forest](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-467/pdf/USREPORTS-467-1.pdf) reiterates that the governing date and the method for measuring value depend on the acquisition procedure.

For land taken under the federal declaration-of-taking procedure, [40 U.S.C. §3114](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title40-section3114) permits title and the right to compensation to vest when the declaration is filed and the estimated compensation is deposited. Final compensation is determined in the proceeding; the court may pay deposited funds on application, enter a deficiency judgment, and issue equitable orders concerning liens. The statute calculates interest from the statutory taking date to payment, with no interest on the part already deposited; [§3116](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A40+section%3A3116+edition%3Aprelim) supplies that subchapter’s rate methodology. These provisions are expressly in Title 40’s land-acquisition subchapter and are not a universal interest formula for every class of property.

In *Kirby Forest*, the Supreme Court distinguished a land declaration-of-taking procedure, where filing and deposit vest title, from “straight-condemnation” proceedings in which the United States does not acquire title or possession until payment. The Court treated the date of taking and the date used for appraisal as separate questions; where substantial delay caused the appraisal date to understate value at the taking date, the court could reassess value on remand. It also explained that interest is required when payment is delayed after a taking to place the owner in an equivalent pecuniary position. Those holdings concern the land procedures and facts before the Court; the decision does not announce a property-type-neutral timing rule for satellites or all tangible personal property. [Kirby Forest, 467 U.S. 1](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-467/pdf/USREPORTS-467-1.pdf).

### 2. Rule 71.1 supplies procedure, not a universal date or interest rate

The current [Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 71.1](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-civil-procedure.pdf) governs federal proceedings to condemn real and personal property. It requires the complaint to identify the authority, use, property and interests; requires known or reasonably ascertainable interest holders to be joined before a compensation hearing; and allows defendants to present compensation evidence and share in an award even if they did not previously appear.

Rule 71.1(j) requires a court deposit when another law makes it a condition of eminent domain, permits deposits when statute allows them, and directs the court and counsel to expedite distribution and determination/payment of compensation. If the final award differs from the amount distributed, it provides for a deficiency or overpayment judgment. It does not set a calendar-day deadline, prescribe a universal taking date, or establish an interest rate. [Rule 71.1(e), (h), (j)](https://www.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/document/federal-rules-of-civil-procedure.pdf).

### 3. Bill v1.10 protections are substantial; §106(a) leaves one drafting ambiguity

Bill v1.10 §103(d) requires an appraisal record to state a valuation date, and §103(g) requires updates before a binding commitment and before closing. Those provisions govern the appraisal and offer record; they do not say that the appraisal date is the legal taking date. Section 105(d) applies Rule 71.1 except where the Act expressly provides otherwise. Section 105(e) prohibits transfer of title or possession of tangible personal property until the court sustains authority and public use, determines compensation, and orders the interest and possession date. Section 105(f) permits Title 40 procedures only for qualifying real-property interests and only according to their terms.

Section 106(a) already guarantees the full amount constitutionally required, including any required interest, and says an estimate or deposit does not extinguish liability. Section 106(b) applies constitutional law to compensation; §106(c) protects disputed proceeds and permits withdrawal of undisputed deposits; §106(d) consents to suit for compensation and interest. Read together with §§103 and 105, those clauses provide substantial safeguards.

The remaining drafting risk is modest but material: §106(a) does not expressly direct the court to identify the taking date and applicable valuation date for each acquired interest. A date used in an appraisal, offer, schedule, estimate or deposit could be confused with the date that controls under the applicable condemnation route. The risk is clearest where the dates differ, as the land cases demonstrate. The audit found no rule that makes such confusion inevitable and no conclusion that Bill v1.10 would violate the Constitution. The omission is a recordkeeping and interpretive ambiguity, not evidence of a substantive compensation shortfall.

## Narrow revision integrated in Bill v1.11

Section 106(a) now adds: “In an action under section 105, the court shall separately determine the date of taking and the applicable valuation date for each interest under the Constitution and governing law. A date stated in an appraisal record, offer, schedule, estimate, or deposit does not supersede a different date required by the Constitution or governing law.”

The edit tells the court to record the two dates under existing law. It does not set a single date, make the dates identical, prescribe a new valuation formula, import 40 U.S.C. §3116 to personal property, guarantee a particular interest rate, set a payment deadline, or add an appropriation. Existing §105(e)’s personal-property no-quick-take rule, §105(f)’s land-only Title 40 interface, and §106(a)’s full-compensation obligation remain intact.

### Contrary considerations and open questions

- Section 103(d) already requires an appraisal valuation date, while §106(b) incorporates applicable constitutional law. A court could treat the new sentence as redundant; the clarification is adopted to prevent the appraisal date from being mistaken for a legal taking date.
- Land under 40 U.S.C. §3114 may vest on filing plus deposit before final compensation; the proposed §105(e) bars transfer of title or possession of personal property until compensation is determined and paid or deposited. A single statutory date or rate would risk disrupting those distinct procedures.
- Rule 71.1 requires expedition but gives no fixed day-count. No fixed payment timetable is added because the constitutional/legal timing varies by acquisition route and because this review does not choose a new policy deadline or appropriation.
- The record contains no transaction-specific appraisal, ownership schedule, property classification, legal date of taking, deposit amount, final award, or delay facts. No asset-specific compensation conclusion is possible.
- The audit does not resolve the broader public-purpose, statutory authority, territorial situs, valuation, appropriation, judgment-fund, lien-priority, or operational-continuity questions. Prior scope 03.06 creditor/lienholder review and the Declaration of Taking Act audit are cited as bounded prior work, not repeated.
