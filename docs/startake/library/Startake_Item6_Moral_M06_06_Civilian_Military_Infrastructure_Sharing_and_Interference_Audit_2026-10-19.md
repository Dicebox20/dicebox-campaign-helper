## 1. Scope and method

The authenticated M06.06 scope is: **“Risks associated with shared resources, priority claims, and interference among user classes.”** This review asks what public U.S. primary sources govern federal/non-federal spectrum coordination, legally authorized national-security or emergency communications priority, and harmful-interference protections relevant to communications infrastructure shared by civilian and government users. It records what those sources establish and what remains transaction-specific.

The source member is `starlink_moral_research_topics/06_defense_dependence_starshield_and_stewardship_of_security.md`, SHA-256 `4d52d6a2afa0875cd7734ccf198db18f1357100b70007b584769e1508aa7318b`. It was recovered from the authenticated source archive and its member hash was verified. The scope was reserved in canonical tracker v370 and independently read back before research.

This review is limited to published U.S. statutes, FCC/NTIA rules and coordination material, official GovInfo publications, and CISA program descriptions. It does not determine which licenses, frequency assignments, service classifications, TSP/WPS assignments, priority clauses, or interference conditions apply to any particular Starlink, Starshield, or customer service. It makes no factual finding about live network controls, traffic queues, terminals, spectrum use, user identities, classified operations, or contracts.

**Deliberate exclusions:** general commercial congestion, capacity disclosure, and equipment scarcity allocation reviewed under M04.09; outage, uptime, restoration readiness, backup-path, and transition-continuity testing reviewed under M04.08 and M06.05; Starlink/Starshield identity or architecture boundaries under M06.01; classified compartmentalization under M06.02; privileged access under M06.03; oversight under M06.07; and Item 1. The TSP rules’ restoration and preemption provisions are discussed only to explain the legal priority mechanism and its limits, not to audit service continuity.

## 2. Executive findings

1. **“Shared infrastructure” describes several different legal mechanisms.** Federal/non-federal spectrum coordination governs who may operate on particular frequencies and under what conditions ([47 C.F.R. §§2.102–2.103](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2023-title47-vol1/pdf/CFR-2023-title47-vol1-part2-subpartB.pdf)). FCC satellite rules govern certain satellite-system coordination and interference procedures ([47 C.F.R. §§25.261, 25.274](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol2/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol2-part25.pdf)). TSP/WPS and presidential emergency authorities govern specified priority scenarios ([47 C.F.R. Part 64, Appendices A–B](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol3/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol3-part64.pdf); [47 U.S.C. §606](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A47+section%3A606+edition%3Aprelim%29)). The [NTIA Manual §4.17](https://redbook.ntia.gov/view/4-17) adds that shared federal/non-federal bands have no default priority unless the U.S. Table or a specific mutually agreed arrangement provides one. Read together, these sources do not establish a general “military user always wins” rule for every commercial network, band, or service.

2. **Federal and non-federal spectrum use are separately administered and coordinated.** NTIA manages federal spectrum assignments and the FCC regulates non-federal spectrum use; the [2022 FCC–NTIA MOU](https://www.ntia.gov/other-publication/2022/memorandum-understanding-between-fcc-and-ntia) and [47 U.S.C. §922](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title47-section922) provide a joint-planning and coordination framework for shared spectrum. The FCC’s and NTIA’s respective roles do not disappear merely because the United States purchases a commercial communications service or acquires commercial assets. The Bill’s §107 already says federal ownership does not itself resolve the FCC–NTIA boundary.

3. **Rules for cross-use of federal and non-federal frequencies include coordination and interference conditions.** Under [47 C.F.R. §2.102(c)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title47-vol1/pdf/CFR-2024-title47-vol1-sec2-102.pdf), non-federal stations may use certain federal frequencies only following the specified consultation/authorization and coordination steps; harmful interference to federal stations requires the interfering non-federal operation to terminate. Under [47 C.F.R. §2.103(a)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2023-title47-vol1/pdf/CFR-2023-title47-vol1-part2-subpartB.pdf), federal stations may use certain non-federal frequencies only under the applicable FCC/NTIA conditions; harmful interference to non-federal stations requires the interfering federal operation to terminate. Certain listed public-safety-band joint-use cases require licensee approval and mutual agreement. These are frequency- and authorization-specific rules, not a rule that government customers receive priority on all commercial spectrum.

4. **TSP creates real but bounded priority and preemption rules.** The FCC’s [Telecommunications Service Priority system under 47 C.F.R. §64.401 and Part 64 Appendix A](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol3/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol3-part64.pdf) applies to eligible national-security and emergency-preparedness services for which priority treatment is requested, assigned, and approved. TSP primarily covers provisioning and restoration ([47 C.F.R. §64.401 and Part 64 Appendix A](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol3/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol3-part64.pdf)); compulsory treatment is specific to service categories, while priority provisioning/restoration for data, video, and IP-based voice may be offered voluntarily by a provider. TSP can permit preemption of existing services under Part 64 Appendix A, section 6 ([official 2025 CFR text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol3/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol3-part64.pdf)) when necessary to provision or restore an assigned NSEP service: suitable spare services are considered first; non-NSEP services may then be preempted, subject to a provider judgment that enough public-switched services remain available; and lower-priority NSEP services may be preempted if needed. Affected-user consent is not required, and notice is best-effort. Part 64 Appendix A, section 3(c) also gives provider-owned control services and orderwires priority over all other services, including NSEP services, and exempt them from preemption. Whether any of these rules covers a particular satellite broadband product, provider, customer, or contract cannot be inferred from a government customer label.

5. **WPS is a separate, service-specific priority program.** The FCC’s WPS rules in Part 64 Appendix B allow participating providers ([official 2025 CFR text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol3/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol3-part64.pdf)), when necessary, to preempt or degrade certain in-progress NSEP-user communications other than 911 to prioritize eligible WPS communications; providers are not required to offer that feature. [CISA’s WPS description](https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/services/wireless-priority-service-wps) says WPS calls do not preempt calls already in progress or deny the general public’s use of the network. The codified [Part 64 Appendix B rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol3/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol3-part64.pdf) separately permits preemption/degradation of in-progress NSEP-user communications (except 911) in specified circumstances. **Inference:** the difference may reflect a narrower affected-user scope; actual provider implementation still needs verification; neither source establishes that WPS applies to satellite broadband or Starlink.

6. **Presidential emergency powers are distinct from routine priority programs.** [47 U.S.C. §606(a)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A47+section%3A606+edition%3Aprelim%29 authorizes presidential preference or priority directions for communications essential to national defense and security during a war in which the United States is engaged. Other subsections of [§606](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2022-title47/pdf/USCODE-2022-title47-chap5-subchapVI.pdf) establish separate, proclamation-dependent powers over specified radio or wire facilities and provide for just compensation when government use or control is authorized. The statute is not a standing priority assignment for every government customer or commercial service.

7. **Interference protections are not a substitute for service-priority terms.** [47 U.S.C. §333](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A47+section%3A333+edition%3Aprelim%29 prohibits willful or malicious interference with authorized radio communications, including government-operated stations. FCC satellite rules separately prescribe coordination ([47 C.F.R. §§25.261, 25.274](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol2/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol2-part25.pdf)) for certain NGSO fixed-satellite-service systems and a process for investigating, recording, and escalating harmful interference. Those rules address spectrum compatibility and interference response, not which civilian or defense customer is served first during congestion.

8. **Bill v1.14 remains unchanged.** Sections 3, 107, and 401 already establish a commercial-only acquisition perimeter, preservation of regulatory approvals and the federal/non-federal boundary, a staged transition plan that identifies defense and emergency service obligations, exclusion of dedicated Starshield/classified systems, and mission-based critical-service classification. No bill-text change is supported by this bounded source review. A future transaction-specific authorization and service schedule should document any actual spectrum-sharing condition, assigned priority, preemption right, conflict/notification process, and customer or service class affected before a transfer or operating-control change.

## 3. Findings by legal mechanism

### 3.1 Federal/non-federal spectrum coordination

The [FCC–NTIA MOU](https://www.ntia.gov/other-publication/2022/memorandum-understanding-between-fcc-and-ntia) states that NTIA is responsible for authorizing federal spectrum use and the FCC regulates non-federal spectrum use; it sets an interagency coordination framework. Congress’s joint-planning provision calls for the agencies to address federal and non-federal spectrum requirements, allocation actions, and increased shared use that does not cause harmful interference ([47 U.S.C. §922](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title47-section922)). The NTIA Manual states that shared federal/non-federal bands carry no default priority unless the U.S. Table or a specific mutually agreed arrangement says otherwise. This is a regulatory division of responsibility, not a transfer of spectrum title to a commercial buyer. (Sources S1–S3, S14.)

The two cross-use provisions impose reciprocal but not identical conditions. [47 C.F.R. §2.102(c)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title47-vol1/pdf/CFR-2024-title47-vol1-sec2-102.pdf) governs non-federal use of certain federal frequencies and requires consultation with federal agencies, conditions agreed by FCC and NTIA, compliance with NTIA rules, and immediate termination of interfering non-federal operation if harmful interference results. [47 C.F.R. §2.103(a)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2023-title47-vol1/pdf/CFR-2023-title47-vol1-part2-subpartB.pdf) governs federal use of certain non-federal frequencies and requires an FCC finding of necessity for coordination, FCC/NTIA conditions, compliance with FCC service rules, and immediate termination of interfering federal operation if harmful interference results. [Section 2.103(b)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2023-title47-vol1/pdf/CFR-2023-title47-vol1-part2-subpartB.pdf) separately addresses specified federal/non-federal public-safety-band joint-use systems, including licensee approval and a mutual agreement. (Sources S4–S5.)

These provisions do not show that every government use of a commercial satellite service is an exercise of federal spectrum authority. [47 U.S.C. §305](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-118/t47/s305/) addresses radio stations that both belong to and are operated by the United States. A public customer relationship, standing alone, does not establish that a privately operated satellite, gateway, or terminal is a government-owned-and-operated station under that section. The exact station, service, authorization, frequency, and operating arrangement must be identified. (Source S6.)

### 3.2 National-security and emergency priority

TSP under [47 C.F.R. §64.401 and Part 64 Appendix A](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol3/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol3-part64.pdf) is a formal service-priority system, not an unstated privilege that follows from a customer’s government status. The Part 64 appendix makes eligibility dependent on the service and an approved priority assignment. Common carriers and providers of services interconnected with common-carrier services must offer prioritized provisioning and restoration for circuit-switched voice; a provider may voluntarily offer priority provisioning and restoration for data, video, and IP-based voice. Services provided by government or non-common carriers may receive priority under specified interconnection conditions, and other government/non-common-carrier services may be included at the discretion of the users through special arrangements. (Sources S7–S8.)

TSP’s preemption sequence under [Part 64 Appendix A, section 6](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol3/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol3-part64.pdf) is material to the civilian-military sharing question. For the assigned NSEP service categories covered by the appendix, preemption may be used when necessary to provision or restore service. The sequence begins with suitable spare services, then permits preemption of non-NSEP services while requiring a reasonable number of public-switched services to remain available based on the provider’s best judgment, then permits lower-priority NSEP services to be displaced if needed. The appendix requires best-effort notice stating the reason and estimated duration, does not require consent of the displaced user, and directs unresolved provider/user conflicts to DHS. These provisions create an identifiable class-impact and process question whenever a covered service or underlying facility is involved; they do not establish that a Starlink broadband account or network currently carries an approved TSP assignment. (Source S8.)

[Part 64 Appendix A, section 3(c)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol3/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol3-part64.pdf) also creates an operational exception: provider-owned control services and orderwires needed to provision, restore, or maintain the provider’s other services fall outside the TSP authorization process, yet are assigned priority in provisioning/restoration above all services including NSEP and cannot be preempted. Leased provider control services remain within TSP. This rule makes the ownership and operational role of a control service relevant to priority analysis; it does not establish that any particular Starlink control plane or resource receives this treatment. (Source S8.)

WPS has its own rules and use case. [Part 64 Appendix B](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol3/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol3-part64.pdf) permits preemption or degradation of in-progress communications from NSEP users (except public-safety 911 communications) when necessary to prioritize eligible WPS communications, but does not require providers to offer preemption or degradation. CISA’s description identifies WPS as a cellular service for authorized devices on participating networks. These provisions must not be collapsed into the TSP circuit-provisioning hierarchy or treated as a rule for satellite broadband. (Sources S8–S9.)

[47 U.S.C. §606](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A47+section%3A606+edition%3Aprelim%29 is a separate statutory layer. The wartime priority power in subsection (a) requires a war in which the United States is engaged and presidential direction. Subsections (c) and (d) use distinct proclamation and subject-matter triggers for radio and wire facilities, respectively; the radio provision includes government use/control upon just compensation. Accordingly, emergency or defense priority should be linked to the actual authority, trigger, order, and covered carrier/facility rather than assumed from a contract label. (Source S10.)

### 3.3 Harmful-interference safeguards

[47 U.S.C. §333](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A47+section%3A333+edition%3Aprelim%29 is limited by its terms to willful or malicious interference with radio communications of a station licensed or authorized under the Communications Act or operated by the United States. It is not a general guarantee against accidental interference, congestion, degraded throughput, outages, or customer competition. The more specific allocation and licensing rules must also be checked. (Source S11.)

For NGSO fixed-satellite-service operations within its stated scope, [47 C.F.R. §25.261](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol2/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol2-part25.pdf) requires good-faith coordination of commonly authorized frequencies. If systems do not coordinate and interference raises an affected receiver’s system-noise-temperature ratio above 6 percent, the rule supplies a default division procedure; separate provisions address earlier-round system protection. The rule is limited to qualifying NGSO FSS systems under an FCC license or U.S. market-access grant. It is a satellite-network sharing rule, not a rule assigning priority among the satellite operator’s civilian and military customers. The official [2025 CFR text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol2/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol2-part25.pdf) and [2025 Federal Register correction](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-01-22/pdf/FR-2025-01-22.pdf) were reviewed; the correction delayed specified amendments to paragraphs (d) and (e), so no delayed amendment is relied on here. (Source S10.)

[47 C.F.R. §25.274](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol2/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol2-part25.pdf) provides an operational interference-investigation path: the earth-station operator checks local equipment and potential sources, contacts the satellite control center, keeps records of interference incidents and resolution for FCC access on request, and cooperates with FCC investigation. This supports a documentation and escalation expectation for covered licensed satellite operations; it does not decide access priority or guarantee uninterrupted service. (Source S10.)

As a deliberately narrow counterexample to any “no government priority exists” claim, [47 C.F.R. §25.260](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title47-vol2/pdf/CFR-2025-title47-vol2-part25.pdf) addresses a specific DoD/non-voice NGSO satellite time-sharing arrangement in 400.15–401 MHz and requires frequency-change capability and demonstrations for the covered licensees. Its band, service, and system-specific text cannot be generalized to Starlink or to other shared satellite infrastructure without matching authorization and band records. (Source S10.)

## 4. Practical risk and evidence matrix

| Mechanism | What the public rule can do | User-class or sharing risk | Facts needed before applying it to a specific service |
|---|---|---|---|
| FCC–NTIA coordination; 47 C.F.R. §§2.102–2.103 | Authorize specified federal/non-federal cross-use with coordinated conditions and interference stop rules | An operation may have to cease when it causes harmful interference; an agency or buyer cannot assume that commercial ownership removes the coordination boundary | Band, station, authorization, licensee, frequency assignment, coordination record, conditions, and any required consent |
| TSP, 47 C.F.R. §64.401 and Part 64 Appendix A | Prioritize eligible service provisioning/restoration; allow limited preemption under an approved priority assignment | A non-NSEP service or lower-priority NSEP service may be displaced; provider-owned control services have a distinct protected priority | Service classification, eligibility basis, assigned priority, requests/approvals, provider participation, underlying-facility flow-down, preemption and notice rules |
| WPS, Part 64 Appendix B | Prioritize eligible wireless priority communications; optional preemption/degradation features for specified NSEP communications | NSEP user communications can be affected under the rule’s specified conditions; providers need not implement preemption | Whether the service is participating cellular WPS, subscribers/priority levels, provider implementation, applicable exceptions |
| 47 U.S.C. §606 | Allow the President to direct wartime communications preference and exercise specified emergency powers over facilities | Orders can alter ordinary service use, but only when statutory triggers and directions apply | Presidential proclamation/order, statutory subsection, covered carrier/facility, duration, compensation and implementing rules |
| 47 C.F.R. §§25.261, 25.274 | Coordinate specified satellite systems and investigate/document harmful interference | Shared frequency operation can require technical coordination or mitigation; no customer-service order is established | FCC license/market access, common bands, processing rounds, coordination agreements, interference records and applicable conditions |

The critical transaction records are therefore: (1) a current FCC authorization and NTIA frequency-assignment/coordination schedule for each affected station, frequency, and function; (2) any TSP/WPS priority request, eligibility decision, assigned level, provider response, and flow-down arrangement; (3) the customer and provider contract terms for priority, preemption, termination, notice, dispute escalation, and affected classes; and (4) relevant FCC interference conditions, records, and remediation commitments. Public sources reviewed here do not supply those Starlink-specific records.

## 6. Limitations, contrary evidence, and open items

- **Contrary evidence to blanket priority claims:** §§2.102(c) and 2.103(a) place interference-related stop duties on the operation causing harmful interference in the specific cross-use scenarios; §25.260 gives an example of a narrow, frequency-specific DoD sharing obligation; WPS is service-specific; and §606(a) is trigger- and order-dependent. These sources refute a blanket military-over-civilian rule.
- **Evidence supporting real priority conflicts:** TSP expressly allows defined service preemption without affected-user consent for covered NSEP provisioning/restoration and gives provider-owned control services a distinct protected position. WPS separately permits limited preemption/degradation of in-progress NSEP communications. These rules make class eligibility, assigned priority, facility ownership, and notice/appeal records material questions.
- **No provider-specific finding:** No FCC/NTIA station or frequency record, TSP/WPS assignment, commercial service contract, priority or preemption schedule, interference agreement, or operational log for a Starlink service was examined. No finding is made about Starlink’s present treatment of government and civilian users.
- **Currency boundary:** The review used the 2025 annual CFR text, the official Title 47 List of CFR Sections Affected through July 2026, and targeted Federal Register checks available on 19 October 2026. The July 2026 list shows no amendments to 47 C.F.R. §§2.102, 2.103, 25.261, or 64.401 or Part 64 Appendix A during its coverage period. Later changes were not exhaustively certified. (Source S11.) The 2025 Federal Register correction delaying §25.261(d)–(e) amendments was considered.
- **Retrieval limitations:** The Legal Data Hunter daily limit was reached before this assignment’s legal-source searches; no Legal Data Hunter verification was completed or retried. Direct official sources were used instead. Some House/NTIA/CISA pages returned maintenance or access errors, or had no extractable lines, through the retrieval interface, although official search-result text was available; GovInfo CFR PDFs and the July 2026 LSA were opened and inspected. This report is not LDH-verified, counsel-reviewed, classified, or engineering-reviewed.
- **No formal acceptance:** M06.06, the parent M06 topic, and Practical Item 6 remain formally unchecked. This work changes no source custody, original scope wording, or acceptance status.

### Open verification questions

1. For each affected station, terminal, gateway, or satellite function, which FCC authorization or market-access grant applies, which federal frequency assignment applies, and which agency/authorization conditions control?
2. Are any services or underlying facilities subject to a current TSP priority assignment, a WPS participation/priority assignment, a special arrangement for a government or non-common-carrier service, or a presidential direction? Who approved the assignment and what is its effective period?
3. Which resource is being prioritized: spectrum access, provisioning/restoration, a cellular call, provider control service, terminal/equipment allocation, or in-service packet capacity? Which existing user classes may be displaced, and what notice, conflict, appeal, and records rules apply?
4. What are the relevant FCC/NTIA coordination agreements, interference conditions and logs, and the authorized technical path to diagnose, mitigate, or cease operations if interference occurs?
5. Do the actual customer, procurement, or transition contracts add service priorities or protections, and do they bind the necessary underlying providers and facilities?
