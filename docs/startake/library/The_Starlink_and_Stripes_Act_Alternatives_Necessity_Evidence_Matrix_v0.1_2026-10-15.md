# The Starlink and Stripes Act — Alternatives and Necessity Evidence Matrix v0.1

**Evidence date:** 15 October 2026. **Assignment:** `STARTAKE-20261015-WORK-ALT-5c47b1`. **Scope:** Item 7 and authenticated research topic 15, “Alternatives and necessity test,” only. This is direct primary-source review and legislative synthesis, not a legal opinion, operational test, cost estimate, or formal acceptance determination.

## Finding

The public record reviewed here establishes several bounded tools that can address particular service, procurement, emergency, resilience, and public-safety needs. It does not establish that any one tool can deliver the same mission, population, rights, duration, or legal control as a publicly owned commercial Starlink network. Nor does it show that compulsory acquisition is the least costly or most effective choice. The alternatives therefore remain **unranked**. Price, speed, access, international trust, cybersecurity, continuity, and legal cost are largely unmeasured on a common basis.

The best-supported non-acquisition comparator is a **targeted public overlay procured from private operators**, illustrated by FirstNet’s mission-specific public-safety network. That design shows one way to create public governance and operational requirements while contracting with a private network operator; its spectrum, mission, population, service technology, and funding model are not comparable to commercial Starlink. This evidence supports further option testing, not a finding that an overlay can replace Starlink.

The current draft bill already requires a completed alternative comparison and a reasoned public-benefit determination before closing. Its §404(a) names contracts, multivendor redundancy, regulatory conditions, competition, and public construction; it does not expressly enumerate every original 15.01–15.08 tool, including triggered emergency operation, notice/appeal obligations, a public critical overlay, and separate negotiated versus compulsory acquisition. A later, separately scoped bill revision should make the original options explicit and preserve the ten criteria below. No bill text was changed in this research assignment.

## Scope, method, and evidence labels

The authenticated original topic 15 calls for comparison of (1) binding government service-level contracts, (2) stronger federal public-service obligations, (3) notice and appeal procedures, (4) emergency communications authorities when legally triggered, (5) resilience procurement and competition, (6) a publicly owned critical overlay, (7) negotiated purchase, and (8) specifically authorized compulsory acquisition. It requires assessment against security, access, international trust, price, speed, enforceability, political interference, cyber risk, continuity, and legal cost, with a transparent impact/tradeoff matrix rather than a predetermined winner. This report follows those eight options and ten criteria; it does not create a new completion denominator.

**Evidence labels:** **Verified** means a primary source establishes the cited statute, rule, or agency/company disclosure. It does not prove Starlink-specific performance or legal application. **Inference** identifies a limited conclusion drawn from the scope of that source. **Unknown** means comparable public evidence was not found or was not measured in this assignment. No numeric score is assigned where a common mission and data baseline are absent.

The saved *Comparable and Replacement-Cost Evidence v0.1* is cross-referenced for OneWeb and other satellite-program observations; those observations are not duplicated or treated as proof of equivalent coverage, cost, capacity, or substitutability. No private Starlink contract, commercial cost forecast, asset-title inventory, current acquisition offer, or actual alternative bid was available in the sources reviewed. Legal Data Hunter was unavailable because its session quota had been reached; statutes and cases below were checked directly against official sources. This is not independent counsel review.

## Option-by-option impact and tradeoff matrix

| Option | Verified legal or program anchor | Impact signals supported by the record | Material unknowns and tradeoffs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **15.01 Public-service regulation and obligations** | 47 U.S.C. §201 imposes reasonable-request and just-and-reasonable service/charge duties on interstate or foreign wire/radio **common carriers**. Section 214 contains certificate rules for covered lines and a service-discontinuance safeguard, with temporary/emergency exceptions. Section 254 sets universal-service policy and support mechanisms; §1302 directs the FCC and state commissions to encourage timely broadband deployment and competition. ([S1](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title47-section201); [S2](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A47+section%3A214%28e%29+edition%3Aprelim%29); [S3](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A47+section%3A254+edition%3Aprelim%29); [S4](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A47+section%3A1302+edition%3Aprelim%29)) | **Inference:** a properly authorized and applicable service rule could target access, nondiscrimination, notice, or continuity without transferring title. Section 214 is a specific service/line control, not general public ownership. | Application depends on service and carrier classification, geography, facility, and current FCC/court status. On 31 July 2026, the Sixth Circuit granted en banc rehearing in *Ohio Telecom Ass’n v. FCC*, vacated its prior panel judgment, stayed the mandate, and restored the case as pending; the earlier panel opinion is not a final controlling disposition. ([S5](https://www.opn.ca6.uscourts.gov/opinions.pdf/26a0211p-06.pdf)) The official opinion index reviewed for this report lists no later published merits opinion through 14 October, but this was not a docket-sheet search. Classification, rulemaking time, enforcement, compliance cost, service effects, cross-border acceptance, and litigation expense remain unresolved. No automatic application of §§201 or 214 to Starlink is assumed. |
| **15.02 Government service contracts and remedies** | 41 U.S.C. §6305 generally bars transfer of a federal contract or order, subject to specified assignment rules. FAR 42.1204 permits agency recognition of a successor in interest in qualifying asset transfers when in the Government’s interest; FAR 16.505 provides fair-opportunity procedures for orders under multiple-award contracts, subject to exceptions. ([S6](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title41-section6305); [S7](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/42.1204); [S8](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/16.505)) | **Verified:** government procurement can define service levels, security terms, remedies, competition, and successor review for the contracts and users it covers. **Inference:** targeted contract amendments or new competitive awards may address specified government missions without acquiring the retail network. | This does not create a universal retail-service duty or public title. The actual Starlink agency contracts, security annexes, termination/step-in rights, service metrics, prices, users, and international terms were not obtained. Actual coverage, amendment/competition time, remedy effectiveness, contract disputes, procurement cost, and continuity through a provider failure remain unknown. |
| **15.03 Triggered emergency authority / temporary operation** | 47 U.S.C. §606(c) permits presidential action after a qualifying proclamation—war or threat, public peril/disaster, other national emergency, or neutrality—to suspend or amend specified radio rules, close certain stations/devices, or authorize government use/control of a qualifying station/device or its apparatus, **upon just compensation**. ([S9](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A47+section%3A606+edition%3Aprelim%29)) | **Verified:** existing law contains a trigger-bound route for temporary control of covered communications equipment with compensation. **Inference:** it may support a bounded contingency plan if facts and jurisdiction meet the statutory conditions. | It is not shown to authorize ordinary-course, permanent acquisition of the global commercial constellation or to ensure a continuing civilian service obligation. The qualifying trigger, covered assets, territorial reach, implementation speed, actual operational readiness, international consequences, and compensation amount need expert review. Executive discretion, cyber access, rollback, continuity after the emergency, and litigation cost have not been measured. |
| **15.04 Competition, procurement diversification, and redundancy** | The FCC National Broadband Map shows provider-reported fixed availability by technology—including satellite—and maximum advertised speeds, and supports location/availability challenges. ([S10](https://help.bdc.fcc.gov/hc/en-us/articles/10467446103579-How-to-Use-the-FCC-s-National-Broadband-Map)) | **Verified:** the map is a useful geographic screening source and a challenge process exists. **Inference:** multivendor procurement and tested failover can reduce dependence where providers and independent paths actually serve a mission. The saved comparables report contains limited peer-program evidence, not equivalent-service proof. | Advertised availability is not mission-level throughput, latency, congestion, terminal compatibility, uptime, priority, cyber resilience, geographic capacity, or independent backhaul/power. Independent alternatives and their costs, procurement lead time, switching friction, international rights, market concentration, and failure correlation require site-specific tests and current bids. Map coverage alone cannot establish a substitute. |
| **15.05 Publicly owned critical overlay** | Congress directed FirstNet to hold a dedicated public-safety license and deploy a nationwide public-safety broadband network using open, transparent, competitive requests to private-sector builders/operators; the statute encourages use of existing commercial wireless infrastructure when economically desirable and requires security/resiliency measures. ([S11](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title47-section1426)) The Authority’s FY2025 annual report says Congress allocated $7 billion and 20 MHz of dedicated spectrum, a 25-year AT&T build/operate/maintain contract followed competitive procurement, and the initial buildout was completed on time and on budget in 2023. The report also says fee payments fund the Authority and that the program operates at no additional taxpayer cost; these are **issuer-reported claims**, not independently validated here. ([S12](https://firstnet.gov/sites/default/files/FirstNetAuthority_AnnualReport_FY2025_V1.pdf)) | **Verified:** a public authority/private operator model has been used for a defined national public-safety mission, including dedicated spectrum, a long-term service contract, security/resilience duties, and deployable capacity. **Inference:** a narrower public overlay could provide accountable capacity for selected critical missions while using private-sector infrastructure. | FirstNet is public-safety focused and uses terrestrial mobile spectrum; it is not a commercial consumer satellite network, does not provide equivalent orbital assets or public ownership of AT&T, and its reported funding/result cannot be scaled as a Starlink cost estimate. Overlay geography, user eligibility, capacity, procurement price, sovereignty/trust, deployment time, cyber tests, residual dependence, and lifecycle/replacement cost remain unknown. |
| **15.06 Negotiated Starlink asset purchase** | The option is identified in the authenticated research scope and in the current proposed bill’s acquisition architecture; no public current offer, price, or seller commitment was located for this assignment. The saved appraisal and comparables reports identify the data and valuation limits. | **Inference:** a willing-seller agreement could define a staged transfer, retained services, transition support, compensation, and liability allocation in contract, potentially reducing some contested-taking issues. It could also preserve negotiated dependency on the seller during transition. | Willingness, exact perimeter, title and consent, price, financing, regulatory/foreign permissions, terms, speed, cyber transition, service continuity, and litigation exposure are all **unknown**. No price or feasibility finding is made. |
| **15.07 Specifically authorized compulsory acquisition** | The integrated bill v0.3 proposes a **new delegation**, including a limited judicial-condemnation route for identified domestic real property or tangible personal property after a supported offer and negotiation period. This is a proposed bill provision, not existing authority or an adjudicated constitutional result. | **Proposed:** a specifically enacted power could supply an explicit public-use process and court-supervised compensation for assets within its scope. The public title objective is most direct if lawful authority reaches the necessary assets. | The draft’s limited domestic tangible-property mechanism does not itself solve equity, orbital, foreign, intellectual-property, software, license, consent, or shared-asset rights. Constitutional basis, public use, necessity, fair compensation, valuation, seller claims, operations, international trust, delay, cyber handover, continuity, and legal expense require independent counsel, appraisers, technical experts, and actual records. No conclusion that the current draft is sufficient is offered. |
| **15.08 Necessity and proportionality determination** | This is a decision standard, not an operating alternative. GAO’s Cost Estimating and Assessment Guide calls for a defined purpose/scope/schedule, technical baseline, work breakdown structure, assumptions, data, methods, sensitivity/risk analysis, documentation, updates using actual costs, and analysis of alternatives. ([S13](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-195g)) | **Proposed:** the bill’s §§104(6) and 404 require a reviewable alternatives comparison and reasoned explanation of expected public benefits, why the selected perimeter/transaction is justified in light of lawful alternatives, and how contrary evidence is addressed. This preserves the acquisition objective while making the adverse case testable. | The necessary baseline is not yet populated: mission, geography, eligible population, service level, capacity, start date, duration, permissions, rights protections, lifecycle costs, risk tolerance, and common price/date conventions. No option can presently be shown to meet or fail the same requirements. |

## Cross-option criteria coverage matrix

| Original criterion | What the primary sources establish now | Evidence needed before a comparative finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Security** | FirstNet law and reporting establish security/resilience requirements for that public-safety network; this does not measure Starlink or the other options. | Threat model, control baseline, classified/unclassified boundary, independent test results, incident response, supply-chain risk, and mission-specific performance for each option. |
| **Access** | Sections 201, 214, 254 and 1302 contain differently scoped service, universal-service, and deployment provisions. FCC mapping supplies reported availability, not actual service quality. | Defined eligible populations and locations; affordability, priority, capacity, service quality, terminal access, actual outages and denials, and enforceable remedy for each option. |
| **International trust** | No source reviewed establishes foreign acceptance of any Starlink acquisition or alternative. FirstNet is a domestic public-safety example. | Country-by-country permissions, registration, spectrum and data rules, customer and allied-government response, foreign enforcement, and service-loss consequences. |
| **Price** | FirstNet’s historical statutory allocation and issuer-reported funding model are contextual only; peer program quotients in the saved COMPS report are not Starlink prices. | Comparable present-value and undiscounted fiscal/resource-cost schedules, acquisition/transition/operations/replacement/security/access costs, liabilities, uncertainty, and funding authority. |
| **Speed** | FirstNet reports its initial buildout completed in 2023 after a 2017 award; that is one historical program outcome, not a forecast for a new overlay or acquisition. | Critical-path schedules with approvals, procurement, build/transfer, staffing, integration, testing, contingencies, and transition milestones for every option. |
| **Enforceability** | Federal statutes and FAR establish powers/processes within their defined scope; the pending Sixth Circuit rehearing makes the broadband-classification issue unsuitable for a settled-rule assumption. | Current authoritative regulatory classification, exact contracts, proposed service standards, jurisdiction, remedies, review routes, and counsel analysis. |
| **Political interference** | The FirstNet statute/report describe a public authority and board; no comparative empirical evidence about political interference or safeguards was found. | Appointment/removal design, agency discretion, transparency, independent oversight, user remedies, procurement controls, and documented governance risks for each option. |
| **Cyber risk** | FirstNet’s statute requires safety, security, resiliency, and cyberattack protection/monitoring for its network. No equivalent comparative test set was available. | Independent red-team and operational tests, key custody, software/update control, identity/data segregation, incident response, and correlated-failure analysis. |
| **Continuity** | Section 214’s discontinuance process is contingent on statutory scope; FAR permits contract-specific successor recognition; FirstNet reports deployable public-safety capacity. None proves continuity for the proposed transfer. | Service-level and restoration metrics, independent failover paths, critical dependencies, transition staffing, migration, spare capacity, rollback, permission sequencing, and witnessed tests. |
| **Legal cost** | Statutes identify bounded authority and process, but no option-specific litigation or implementation-cost estimate was found. | Counsel-reviewed claim map, case scenarios, foreign and domestic proceedings, administrative and court schedules, damages/compensation exposure, and contingency estimates. |

## Legislative and research implications

1. Preserve §104’s no-close conditions and §404’s common-basis, lifecycle-cost, uncertainty, dissent, and public-response requirements. The evidence reviewed does not support deleting the alternatives gate or selecting an option by assertion.
2. In a separate legislative-revision assignment, crosswalk the enacted text explicitly to each original 15.01–15.08 option. In particular, identify service-level remedies and notice/appeal; the narrow conditions for emergency authority; targeted public overlays; negotiated purchase; and the proposed, court-supervised compulsory fallback. Keep the ten original criteria visible.
3. Build the comparison around a mission specification and independently tested service requirements. Where options provide different rights or services, show the gap rather than converting the comparison into cost-per-satellite or cost-per-user arithmetic.
4. Carry open data forward as named inputs: live government contract terms; current regulatory status and counsel analysis; site-level competing-service measurements and bids; overlay design and lifecycle estimate; negotiated seller position; exact asset/perimeter and permission schedules; and actual transition/security cost. The existing financial workbook and COMPS report remain unchanged.
